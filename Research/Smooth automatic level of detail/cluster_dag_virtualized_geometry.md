## 1. Build: how the cluster DAG is made

### Takeaway
The build is a bottom-up loop: cut the mesh into clusters of at most 128 triangles, then repeatedly group neighbouring clusters, merge each group, simplify it to half its triangles with only the group's outer border locked, and split the result into new 128-triangle clusters, until one root cluster is left. Groups are chosen afresh at every level by graph partitioning (METIS) so that a border locked at one level lies inside a group at the next; that regrouping is what makes the structure a DAG and stops locked edges from piling up. The loop and its reasons are confirmed from the Deck; the group size (8 to 32 clusters) is confirmed only from a secondary source, and the exact error and sphere bookkeeping only from memory.

### Cited Findings
- `[P]` The build loop: clusters are grouped, the group's triangles are merged and simplified to about half the triangle count, and the simplified triangle list is split back into clusters of 128 triangles; this repeats "until everything has been reduced to a single cluster at the root of the DAG". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Cluster size: a cluster is 128 triangles; the Deck says the number of triangles per cluster should be as close to, but no greater than, 128 "to fill the rasterizer's waves". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[S]` A university lecture deck gives the same figures plus the group size: each cluster holds 128 triangles ("to fit vertex processing memory cache"), each group holds 8 to 32 clusters, the group is simplified to 50 % of its triangles and split again per 128 triangles. The search tool could not find "8–32" in the Deck itself. — [LMU Munich lecture slides](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/lehre//ws2122/gp/slides/gp-ws2122-extra-nanite.pdf)
- `[S-weak]` One blog adds that 128 is a hard limit because the visibility buffer stores a 7-bit triangle index (single unconfirmed source, but consistent with the 30/27/7 bit packing in section 4). — [traceyang.com walkthrough, June 2026](https://traceyang.com/en/2026/06/11/nanite-end-to-end-pipeline-walkthrough-qa/)
- `[S]` How the leaf clusters are formed: Nanite uses METIS to split the triangle set recursively in half until the parts are close to the maximum size. — [zeux/meshoptimizer discussion #750](https://github.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/discussions/750)
- `[P]` Why borders are locked: if each cluster chose its level independently, neighbouring clusters at different levels would crack; the naive fix is to lock the shared boundary edges during simplification so that independently simplified clusters always match. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Why naive locking in a tree fails: a locked seam between two subtrees stays locked at every level and collects dense triangles; "there will be so much cruft on the boundary between those subtrees that you won't even be able to halve the triangles per level". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` The fix: group adjacent clusters and force the whole group to make the same level decision. The edges that the group's clusters share with each other then no longer need locking and are collapsed like interior edges; only the group's outer border stays locked. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Why it is a DAG: the merged and simplified group is re-split into new clusters that go back into the pool for the next round of grouping, which amounts to "splitting the parent into two, while all children are still connected to both parents". Because of this, "there can't be locked edges that stay locked and collect cruft". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` How groups are chosen: it is a graph-partitioning problem. Graph nodes are clusters; graph edges connect clusters whose triangles touch; edge weights count the shared triangle edges; the minimum edge cut "corresponds to the least number of locked edges across the mesh for this level". The Deck names the METIS library as the solver and notes that the less locking there is, the better the simplifier can work. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` The build is an import-time step: during import, meshes are broken into hierarchical clusters of triangle groups. Importing with Nanite enabled adds processing time, and Epic recommends a shared Derived Data Cache for large projects. — [Docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine)
- `[S]` The open Nanite-like builder in the meshoptimizer project follows the same recipe: cluster size 128, cluster-boundary vertices locked during simplification so they cannot move, and a "simplify threshold" of 0.85. — [deepwiki (auto-generated wiki) on meshoptimizer's Nanite-like LOD system](https://deepwiki.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/3.2-nanite-like-lod-system)
- `[S]` A later mobile system built on the same idea (Tencent, SIGGRAPH 2024) merges and regroups clusters "like UE5's Nanite", records for non-leaf nodes the "errors from merging", and adds a merge coefficient for asset control. — [Tencent NanoMesh slides](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2024/content/Cao-NanoMesh/AdavanceRealtimeRendering_NanoMesh0810.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Reconstructed procedure** (each step tagged with how well it is supported: V = confirmed above, R = recalled only, see Gaps):
  1. Weld the mesh so that triangles sharing an edge are known to be adjacent (R).
  2. Partition the triangle adjacency graph into leaf clusters of at most 128 triangles, as close to 128 as possible, by recursive METIS bisection (V). Leaf error is zero.
  3. Build the cluster adjacency graph, edge weight = number of shared boundary edges (V). Partition it into groups of 8 to 32 clusters, minimising the cut (V for the objective, S for the size).
  4. For each group: merge its triangles; lock the vertices on the group's outer border; simplify by edge collapse to 50 % of the triangles (V); record the error the simplifier reports (R).
  5. Split the simplified patch into clusters of at most 128 triangles (V). These are the parents of every cluster in the group.
  6. Give all parents of the group one shared error (at least the largest error among the children and at least the new simplification error) and one shared bounding sphere that encloses the children's spheres (R). Store the same pair with each child as its "parent error" and "parent sphere" (R).
  7. Repeat from step 3 on the new clusters until one cluster remains (V). Give root clusters an infinite parent error (S, see section 2).
- **Depth and storage follow from halving.** With 128 triangles per cluster and 50 % per level, a mesh of T triangles has about T/128 leaf clusters and about log2(T/128) levels above them: about 11 levels for 200,000 triangles (1,563 leaf clusters) and about 17 levels for 10 million triangles (78,125 leaf clusters). The total triangle count over all levels tends to 2T (1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + ...).
- **What the build needs from its input mesh.** (a) Connectivity: both partitioning steps work on shared-edge adjacency, and edge collapse needs welded vertices, so unwelded "triangle soup" must be welded first. (b) Simplifiability: every level must actually halve. A mesh made of thousands of tiny unconnected pieces (cards, leaves) has little or no shared-edge adjacency and almost every edge is a border, so the simplifier stalls; the open builder's 0.85 threshold is, as I remember its demo source, the test for a group that failed to simplify enough (not confirmed this session). (c) Attribute seams (hard normals, UV borders) add vertices and constrain collapses; flat-faced buildings simplify well geometrically but each hard edge is a seam. These three points are reasoning from the confirmed build steps, not statements found in a source this session.
- **The 128 figure has three independent justifications** in the sources (fills a compute "wave", fits a vertex cache, fits a 7-bit triangle index). A renderer without a software rasteriser is bound by none of them and could choose a different cluster size.

- **Recalled, not re-verified:**

This is what makes both values non-decreasing towards the root. The open reimplementation is believed to add the new error to the children's maximum instead of taking the larger of the two (recalled, medium). → verify against [jglrxavpok's write-up](https://jglrxavpok.github.io/2024/04/02/recreating-nanite-runtime-lod-selection.html) and the meshoptimizer `clusterlod` demo.
  - RECALLED (medium): the Deck credits the idea to earlier work (Quick-VDR, Batched Multi-Triangulation, Ponchio's thesis); see section 8.

## 2. Runtime: selecting the cut

### Takeaway
Each frame Nanite draws a "cut" through the DAG: a cluster is drawn when its own projected error is at or below the pixel threshold and its parent's projected error is above it. Because every cluster carries its own and its parent's error and sphere, the test needs no neighbours and no traversal order, so it runs for all clusters in parallel on the GPU; a hierarchy over the groups, a persistent-thread compute pass and two-pass occlusion culling against a hierarchical depth buffer only make it fast. The rule is confirmed from reimplementations and course notes; the exact wording of Epic's slide, Epic's projection formula and the 1-pixel default are recalled only.

### Cited Findings
- `[P]` "At run time we find a cut of the tree that matches the desired LOD. That means different parts of the same mesh can be at different levels of detail based on what's needed." "This is done in a view dependent way based on the screen space projected error of the cluster." "A parent will draw instead of its children if we determine you couldn't tell the difference from this point of view." — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[S]` The rule as code in a reimplementation: the cluster is rendered if `clusterError <= errorThreshold && parentError > errorThreshold`. — [jglrxavpok, "Recreating Nanite: Runtime LOD selection"](https://jglrxavpok.github.io/2024/04/02/recreating-nanite-runtime-lod-selection.html)
- `[S]` The same rule in the meshoptimizer-based system: select clusters where the self error is at or below the threshold and the parent error is above it; its example threshold is `2e-3` of the screen, described as 2 pixels at 1080p. — [deepwiki summary of meshoptimizer's Nanite-like LOD system](https://deepwiki.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/3.2-nanite-like-lod-system)
- `[S-weak]` One write-up words the parent condition as "at or under" the threshold, which conflicts with the strict "greater than" in the other two; treat the two sources above as correct. — [knnyism, "Nanite at Home"](https://knnyism.com/posts/nanite/)
- `[S]` A cluster with no parent (nothing coarser exists) gets a parent error of infinity, so the root is drawn whenever its own error passes. The digest did not say which reimplementation write-up this came from; most likely — [jglrxavpok](https://jglrxavpok.github.io/2024/04/02/recreating-nanite-runtime-lod-selection.html)
- `[S]` Projection to the screen in a reimplementation: transform the centre of the cluster's bounding sphere to clip space, do the perspective divide, and scale the geometric error by that ratio. Attribution within the result set uncertain; most likely — [jglrxavpok](https://jglrxavpok.github.io/2024/04/02/recreating-nanite-runtime-lod-selection.html)
- `[S]` Monotonic errors are what allow the parallel test: course notes say Nanite adjusts errors so that parent and child errors are ordered and "the cut can be computed efficiently in parallel" (the digest's wording about which side is raised was garbled); another returned write-up says monotonicity keeps the cut well defined. The same notes describe a queue-based GPU traversal in which a job's parent error is checked and its children are pushed if the error is above the threshold. — [University of Illinois CS 418 notes](https://cs418.cs.illinois.edu/website/text/nanite.html)
- `[P]` The pipeline is GPU-driven: "for each view we can determine the visible instances in a single dispatch", and when only depth is needed all visible geometry can be drawn with one indirect draw call. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Cluster culling: triangles are grouped into clusters with bounds, and clusters are culled against the frustum and the hierarchical depth buffer (HZB). Cone-based backface culling of clusters is "not typically worthwhile" because almost all backfacing clusters are already occlusion culled. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Two-pass occlusion culling: use last frame's visibility as the starting point, draw that, build an HZB from it, then test against the new HZB to find what became visible this frame but was not visible last frame. The Deck calls this an almost perfect occlusion culling solution. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[S]` Nanite's two-pass scheme uses what was visible in the previous frame, captured in an HZB, "to massively speed up" the determination of what is visible this frame. — [GameDev Stack Exchange answer (mirror)](https://backiee.wasmer.app/http_gamedev_stackexchange_com/questions/198454/how-does-unreal-engine-5s-nanite-work/202884)
- `[S]` Sebastian Aaltonen says the clustered rendering and two-pass occlusion culling he presented years earlier have become a de-facto standard. The earlier talk is, to my knowledge, "GPU-Driven Rendering Pipelines" (SIGGRAPH 2015), whose link the search returned by title only. — [Aaltonen, SIGGRAPH 2023 Advances](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2023/AaltonenHypeHypeAdvances2023.pdf), [Haar & Aaltonen 2015](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/aaltonenhaar_siggraph2015_combined_final_footer_220dpi.pdf)
- `[S]` Persistent threads in the shipped engine: a `PersistentCull` pass culls both hierarchy nodes and clusters when the console variable `r.Nanite.PersistentThreadsCulling` is on, combining the work of several separate passes. — [Unreal Engine forum, "How some Nanite passes work?"](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/how-some-nanite-passes-work/2667840)
- `[S]` Order of passes in a frame: streaming reads last frame's cluster requests, then `InstanceCull`, then `PersistentCull` over the BVH, then rasterisation (hardware and software), then the HZB build. — [LMU Munich lecture slides](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/lehre//ws2122/gp/slides/gp-ws2122-extra-nanite.pdf)
- `[S]` The hierarchy over the DAG: a BVH whose node bounds accelerate cluster culling; parent error and bounding-sphere data are stored so that child clusters are handled together. — [Vulcanite (University of Pennsylvania CIS 565 project)](https://github.com/bdwhst/Vulcanite)
- `[P]` Streaming uses the same cut idea: "We can at any point mark a cut of the tree as leaves and not store anything past it in RAM." — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Design goal behind the selection: think in clusters drawn on screen and aim to draw the same number of clusters every frame regardless of how many objects there are or how dense they are, so that rendering cost follows screen resolution, not scene complexity. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)

### Inferences
- **A usable projection formula** (standard perspective mathematics, consistent with the reimplementation described above): with object-space error `e`, instance scale `s`, distance `d` from the camera to the nearest point of the error sphere (clamped to the near plane), viewport height `H` in pixels and vertical field of view `fovY`, the error in pixels is `e·s·H / (2·d·tan(fovY/2))`. Draw the cluster if this is at or below the threshold for the cluster's own (error, sphere) pair and above it for the parent pair. The meshoptimizer example threshold of 0.002 of the screen height equals about 2 pixels at 1080p (2/1080 = 0.00185), which checks out.
- **Why the test can run independently per cluster.** Both inputs are stored with the cluster, and the function of the view is deterministic. All clusters produced from one group hold identical "own" data, and all clusters consumed by one group hold identical "parent" data, so every member of a group reaches the same decision without communicating. If the projected error never decreases from child to parent, the predicate "error at or below threshold" flips exactly once along any path from a leaf to the root, so exactly one level is drawn for every piece of surface: nothing is drawn twice and nothing is missing.
- **Why no cracks appear.** A cut can only pass along group borders, because a group's clusters switch together. A group's outer border was locked while that group was simplified, so the border is geometrically identical one level down and one level up. Monotonic errors also rule out the remaining case, a neighbour two levels away across an edge that was not a group border: two clusters produced from the same group share one error value, so one cannot be replaced by its children while the other's parent is already acceptable. This is my reasoning from the confirmed build rules, not a quoted proof.
- **Why a hierarchy over groups is safe.** A cluster is drawn only if its parent error projects above the threshold. If a hierarchy node stores the largest parent error and an enclosing sphere of everything beneath it and that pair projects at or below the threshold, no cluster beneath can pass, so the whole subtree is skipped. The hierarchy is an accelerator; the per-cluster rule alone is already correct.

### Gaps
- **Epic's exact projection function and the default threshold were not confirmed this session.**
  - RECALLED (high): the Deck states the rule on a slide as drawing a cluster when the parent's error is too high and its own error is small enough ("can be evaluated in parallel"), requires the error to be monotonic ("parent view error >= child view error") so that the cut is unique, says the simplifier's error is projected to the screen "corrected for distance and angle distortion at worst-case point in sphere bounds", and says all clusters of a group reach the same decision without communication because the "same input" gives the "same output". → verify in the [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf), slides on runtime and parallel LOD selection.
  - RECALLED (high, UE source and console help): the threshold is `r.Nanite.MaxPixelsPerEdge`, default 1.0, described as the triangle edge length in pixels that the runtime targets; lower engine scalability levels raise it (medium: 2 at "medium", 4 at "low"). `r.Nanite.MinPixelsPerEdgeHW`, default 32, is the edge length above which a cluster goes to the hardware rasteriser (medium).
  - RECALLED (medium, UE source): the engine's projection takes the nearest and farthest points of the LOD sphere, multiplies view depth by the cosine of the angle from the view axis, clamps at the near plane, and compares that scale with `error × instance scale × (0.5 × projection[1][1] × view height) / MaxPixelsPerEdge`.
- **Hierarchy fan-out and persistent-thread speed-up: not confirmed.** RECALLED (low to medium): the Deck's slide describes a BVH with 8 children per node whose nodes store the maximum parent error of their children, with groups' cluster lists at the leaves; later engine source is believed to use 4. RECALLED (medium): persistent threads replace one dispatch per hierarchy level by a single dispatch in which compute threads pull nodes from a shared queue until it is empty; the Deck gives a speed-up figure that I do not remember.
- **How streaming requests feed back from the cut** (a cluster whose children are not loaded acts as a leaf and requests them) is recalled only (medium) and was not confirmed.

## 3. Why transitions are invisible

### Takeaway
Nanite switches levels abruptly, cluster by cluster; nothing found in any source describes geomorphing or cross-fading between cluster levels. The switch is meant to be invisible because the chosen level differs from the next finer one by less than the pixel threshold, and Karis explicitly rejected the earlier geomorph-by-distance solution because it ties detail to distance bands and breaks under streaming. The often-repeated statement that temporal anti-aliasing hides the remainder could not be confirmed in this session.

### Cited Findings
- `[P]` The stated criterion is perceptual: a parent is drawn instead of its children "if we determine you couldn't tell the difference from this point of view". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Before clusters, Karis explored the other approach: a hierarchical-LOD scheme with a hierarchy of triangle-mesh chunks in a sparse octree, a view-dependent cut drawn each frame, and Progressive Buffers geomorphing so that level transitions do not crack. The digests do not establish whether he built it or only designed it. — [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- `[P]` Why he moved on from it: Progressive Buffers avoid cracks by geomorphing as a function of distance, which "assumes everything in a LOD band is loaded"; "If a node at the desired level isn't present there are problems." He compares it to clipmaps and wanted a sparse solution that adapts to visibility and to where the detail is. Morphing also forces every node to be spatially smaller than a LOD band, and "the number of triangles per node has to be highly variable or it will draw and store more triangles than are necessary for flat regions". His summary: "I like the idea of HLOD but I don't like that crack solution." — [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- `[P]` The same objection in the 2021 talk, about schemes where level is a function of distance bands: "This means LOD can't be based on visual error of that node would introduce, meaning it can't be adaptive. It also means that if a LOD isn't resident for a certain portion of the surface it may be unable to obey the band requirement and thus will crack." (One digest attached this passage to the grouping step; the phrase "band requirement" shows it concerns distance-band schemes.) — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` The question that led to clusters: "what's the least knowledge neighboring clusters of triangles must have of one another and still guarantee no cracks?" — [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- `[P]` Progressive meshes and their view-dependent form were rejected as too fine-grained: "making decisions per vertex is excessive". — [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- `[P]` What geomorphing buys in the scheme he rejected: "A rendering method that geomorphs the geometry in the vertex shader using per-vertex weights. This approach completely prevents LOD pops and boundary cracks, while still using 'GPU-friendly' static vertex and index buffers." — [Sander & Mitchell, Progressive Buffers, SIGGRAPH 2006 course chapter](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2006/Chapter1-Out-of-Core_Rendering_of_Large_Meshes_with_Progressive_Buffers.pdf)
- `[S]` Sub-pixel error is the stated reason a pop is not seen, in a 2026 course deck describing Nanite's tessellation: "Integer tess factors, nested splits; Pattern pops when tess factors change; Not visible with sub-pixel triangle error, similar to Reyes". — [SIGGRAPH 2026 Advances, "Adaptive Tessellation and Subdivision"](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2026/content/Adaptive%20Tessellation%20and%20Subdivision_7.pdf)
- `[S-weak]` The far-distance stand-in is not pop-free: a forum post says Nanite's imposters cost memory and can cause popping. — [Unreal Engine forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/nanite-material-switching-based-on-distance-lods-replacement/1247397/5)
- **Other representations Karis rejected, with his reasons:**
  - `[P]` Voxels and implicit surfaces: the most discussed alternative, with many potential advantages. But resampling is costly (a 2-million-polygon bust became 13 million narrow-band distance-field voxels), thin features vanish when thinner than a voxel or leak attributes between their two sides when only a few voxels thick, and replacing explicit surfaces would take many years of compounding research and industry experience. Voxels do avoid cracks, because "voxels are volumetric and can be no thinner than the width of a voxel". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf), [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
  - `[P]` Subdivision surfaces: "subdivision by definition is amplification only", so the base cage cannot be simplified; artists reported that "the cage is very often higher than typical game low polys". His conclusion: artist authoring choices need to be separated from rendering cost. The digest did not say which of the two Karis decks this came from. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf) or [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
  - `[P]` Displacement maps: strong for close-up detail but poor for general simplification. Projecting to normal or displacement maps is effectively uniform resampling, which can damage hard-surface features, and displacement cannot increase a surface's genus (a simple shape cannot be displaced into a chain link). — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
  - `[P]` Points: very fast to draw, but without massive overdraw they need hole filling, and there is no way to tell an intended small gap from a hole. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
  - `[P]` Geometry images: essentially vector displacement maps relative to the origin. Karis's first idea after virtual texturing was "virtual geometry images" (this second point is from a digest that did not name which Karis deck; the HPG deck is the likely one). — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf), [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
  - `[S]` A reader's notes on the keynote add that geometry images and displacement did not suit hard-surface meshes and that geometry images could not handle animated geometry (single secondary source). — [Notes on the HPG 2022 keynote (Chinese)](https://dorgon.horizon-studio.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/nanite_journey_note_by_dorgon.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Nanite does not geomorph or cross-fade between cluster levels.** The selection rule is a yes-or-no test per cluster, no excerpt describes a blend, and Karis dropped the geomorph crack solution in favour of locked group borders. The invisibility of a switch therefore depends entirely on the error estimate being accurate and at or below about one pixel.
- **The property is conditional, not free.** Raise the threshold to 2 or 4 pixels (as a renderer without a software rasteriser will want to, see section 4) and each switch moves the surface by up to that many pixels at once. At that point something else must hide it: temporal accumulation, dithering, or a geomorph.
- **A geomorph is compatible with the DAG in principle** (my reasoning, not found in any source): inside a group, edge collapse gives every fine vertex a destination on the simplified patch, all clusters of the group share one projected parent error from which a common blend factor could be derived, and border vertices are locked so they would not move. It would cost a second position per vertex, and attributes would need the same treatment. Nanite does not do this.

### Gaps
- **The role of temporal anti-aliasing was not confirmed.** RECALLED (medium to low): in the 2021 talk Karis says that with error below a pixel the difference between levels is imperceptible and that temporal anti-aliasing smooths over what small differences remain; I cannot place the slide. → verify in the [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf) and the [recorded talk](https://www.youtube.com/embed/eviSykqSUUw).
- RECALLED (high): Epic's documentation lists among Nanite's benefits "Loss of quality is rare or non-existent, especially with LOD transitions" and that level of detail "is automatically handled". → verify in the [Docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine).
- **Karis's words on discrete LOD specifically were not found.** The HPG deck has a section on progressive meshes and view-dependent progressive meshes that the search tool excerpted only in fragments.
- RECALLED (medium): the Deck stresses that the error estimate must be accurate because it drives selection: underestimating shows as popping, overestimating wastes triangles.

## 4. Rasterisation and shading: what belongs to the LOD algorithm and what does not

### Takeaway
The level-of-detail algorithm ends when the list of visible clusters is known. Everything after it (visibility buffer, software rasteriser, deferred material pass) is a separate optimisation for drawing pixel-sized triangles cheaply. Nanite itself sends clusters with large triangles to the ordinary hardware rasteriser, and independent renderers reuse the same DAG with their own rasterisation (mesh shaders, for example). The software path is reported to be about three times faster than hardware for pixel-sized triangles; a renderer without it pays that factor or draws larger triangles.

### Cited Findings
- `[P]` Visibility buffer: rasterisation writes only depth, an instance ID and a triangle ID per pixel; afterwards a material shader per pixel loads the triangle's data and interpolates the vertex attributes. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[S-weak]` Both rasteriser paths write to one buffer; each pixel holds a 64-bit value updated atomically so that the nearest surface wins; the packing is 30 bits of depth, 27 bits of visible-cluster index and 7 bits of triangle index. — [vrarwiki, "Nanite"](https://vrarwiki.com/wiki/Nanite)
- `[S]` Measured ratio: "On average, Nanite's software rasterizer achieves roughly three times the throughput of the hardware pipeline, with even larger gains for pure micropolygon geometry." Nanite falls back to hardware rasterisation for larger triangles. — [CuRast (TU Wien), arXiv 2604.21749](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.21749)
- `[S]` A second paper reports the same: in scenes with mostly pixel-sized triangles Nanite's software pipeline achieves more than a 3x speed-up, and Nanite chooses between the hardware and software pipelines dynamically. — [Schütz, Kerbl, Wimmer, "Software Rasterization of 2 Billion Points in Real Time", arXiv 2204.01287](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.01287)
- `[S-weak]` The switch-over: clusters whose triangles are under about 32 pixels go to the compute-shader software rasteriser, larger ones to hardware. — [vrarwiki, "Nanite"](https://vrarwiki.com/wiki/Nanite)
- `[S]` The advantage is specific to dense geometry: a 2026 software rasteriser reports gains of 2 to 5 times over Vulkan hardware rasterisation for unique dense geometry, while hardware remains an order of magnitude faster for low-polygon meshes. — [CuRast, arXiv 2604.21749 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21749v1)
- `[S-weak]` Platform requirement: Nanite needs DirectX 12 with Shader Model 6.6, or Vulkan with 64-bit atomics. — [Medium, "Understanding Nanite: When to Use It and When to Skip It"](https://medium.com/@sarah.hyperdense/understanding-nanite-when-to-use-it-and-when-to-skip-it-b7fcfadc3058)
- `[S]` A student renderer without 64-bit atomics stores the visibility buffer as a 64-bit texture split into two 32-bit halves. — [Vulcanite](https://github.com/bdwhst/Vulcanite)
- `[S]` Software rasterisation into a visibility buffer with atomics is now used elsewhere for sub-pixel primitives, for example hair strands in a 2025 title. — [SIGGRAPH 2025 Advances, strand hair in "Indiana Jones and the Great Circle"](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/Strand%20Hair%20in%20IJGC%20-%20Final%20Slides%20(Post-Conference).pdf)
- `[S]` Epic later rebuilt the material pass as GPU-driven, compute-based shading (title and link only; content not read). — [Epic blog, "Take a deep dive into Nanite GPU-driven materials"](https://www.unrealengine.com/blog/take-a-deep-dive-into-nanite-gpu-driven-materials)
- `[P]` Scope of the talk, for orientation: it covers how the data structure is "built, streamed, decompressed, culled, rasterized, and finally shaded". — [SIGGRAPH 2021 Advances course page](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/index.html)
- `[S]` Independent renderers that reuse the DAG with their own rasterisation: a mesh-shader implementation, the meshoptimizer demo, a Vulkan student project, a three.js experiment (the last by title only). — [jglrxavpok, "Recreating Nanite: Mesh shader time"](https://jglrxavpok.github.io/2024/05/13/recreating-nanite-mesh-shader-time.html), [deepwiki on meshoptimizer](https://deepwiki.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/3.2-nanite-like-lod-system), [Vulcanite](https://github.com/bdwhst/Vulcanite), [three-nanite README](https://github.com/AIFanatic/three-nanite/blob/master/README.md)

### Inferences
- **Essential to the LOD algorithm:** the DAG build (section 1), the stored error and sphere pairs, and the per-cluster test (section 2). **Needed for scale but not for correctness:** the hierarchy over groups and some form of occlusion culling. **Separate optimisations a simpler renderer can omit:** the software rasteriser, the visibility buffer and deferred material pass, persistent threads, page streaming and Nanite's compression. The evidence is that Nanite's own hardware path draws clusters through the ordinary rasteriser and that independent renderers reuse the DAG with rasterisation of their own.
- **What an implementation without a software rasteriser loses.** (1) Roughly a factor of three in rasterisation throughput on clusters of pixel-sized triangles, by the figures above; those figures come from desktop GPUs, and nothing found this session measures tile-based mobile GPUs. (2) The practical remedy is a larger threshold, so that triangles are several pixels across; triangle count falls roughly with the square of the edge length. (3) With a threshold above a pixel the "invisible switch" argument of section 3 no longer holds on its own.
- **The 7-bit triangle index and the 64-bit atomic** are properties of the software rasteriser's buffer. A hardware-rasterised renderer needs neither, and so is not bound to 128 triangles per cluster or to 64-bit atomics.

### Gaps
- **Why hardware rasterisers are inefficient for pixel-sized triangles** was not retrieved from the Deck. RECALLED (medium to high): the Deck explains that hardware rasterisers are built for large triangles (coarse tile binning, 4×4 micro-tiles, output in 2×2 pixel quads), are parallel over pixels rather than triangles, and set up only a few triangles per clock; and states that Epic's software rasteriser is 3x faster than the fastest primitive-shader implementation they could build. → verify in the [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf), rasterisation slides.
- RECALLED (medium to high): the software rasteriser runs one compute thread group of 128 per cluster, transforms vertices once, then rasterises one triangle per thread over its bounding rectangle, and depth-tests with a 64-bit atomic maximum on the packed value.
- RECALLED (medium, UE source): where mesh shaders are unavailable, Nanite's hardware path draws each visible cluster as one instance of a fixed 128-triangle draw, and the vertex shader fetches positions from the cluster data by vertex index. This is the path most relevant to an API without mesh shaders; not confirmed.
- **Material pass details and the engine version that introduced compute-based shading** were not confirmed. RECALLED (medium): originally one full-screen pass per material, restricted by a depth-equals test on a material-ID depth buffer and a coarse tile grid; replaced by compute-based shading around 5.4.
- **Whether shader-language atomics in the target API are wide enough** for a packed depth-plus-ID value was deliberately not searched; it must be checked by whoever covers the target platform.

## 5. Numbers

### Takeaway
Confirmed figures this session: about 5.6 bytes per stored Nanite triangle on disk (2021 talk, via a 2026 citation), 14.4 bytes per input triangle or about 13.8 MB per million input triangles (Epic's documentation, via quotations), a hard cap of 16 million instances per scene, and Epic's statement that the number of triangles drawn is proportional to the number of pixels. Build time, page size, frame-time and per-frame triangle counts could not be confirmed.

### Cited Findings
- `[S]` Disk size per stored triangle: a 2026 course deck attributes to Karis et al. 2021 "5.6 bytes per Nanite triangle" on disk. The same deck estimates uncompressed mesh data at about 44 bytes per triangle (12 bytes of connectivity plus 32 bytes of vertex data, at roughly 64 bytes per vertex), and shows a second figure of 2.3 bytes per triangle whose meaning the digest could not determine. — [SIGGRAPH 2026 Advances, "Adaptive Tessellation and Subdivision"](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2026/content/Adaptive%20Tessellation%20and%20Subdivision_7.pdf)
- `[S]` Disk size per input triangle: posts quoting Epic's documentation give an average of 14.4 bytes per input triangle, "an average one million triangle Nanite mesh will be ~13.8 megabytes (MB) on disk". A forum thread attributes the figure to the Valley of the Ancient sample. The digest did not name which returned post carried the quotation; the NeoGAF thread's title matches. — [NeoGAF thread on the UE5 docs](https://neogaf.com/threads/ue-5-docs-confirms-nanite-assets-can-actually-be-smaller-than-traditional-ones.1607105/), [Beyond3D thread](https://forum.beyond3d.com/goto/post?id=2205933)
- `[S-weak]` Total for the first demo: viewers' notes of a Karis livestream report all Nanite data on disk, compressed, as 6.14 GB. — [ResetEra thread](https://www.resetera.com/threads/epic-games-brian-karis-runs-unreal-engine-5-ps5-demo-on-his-pc.438958/)
- `[P]` Triangles drawn against resolution: Epic's documentation says Nanite scales well with screen resolution thanks to fine-grained level of detail and occlusion culling, and that regardless of the geometric complexity of the source data, the number of triangles Nanite attempts to draw is always proportional to the number of pixels (my translation of the Chinese edition of the page returned by the search). — [Docs: Working with Nanite-enabled content](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/working-with-naniteenabled-content?lang=zh-CN)
- `[P]` The same goal in the talk: draw the same number of clusters every frame regardless of object count or density; cost should follow screen resolution, not scene complexity. — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Instances: the documentation lists a hard cap of 16 million Nanite instances per scene. — [Docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-US/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine)
- `[P]` Instances scale linearly, not sub-linearly: "linear scaling in instances can be ok, at least within the limit of the scale of the level you typically want loaded around you". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[S]` Threshold used by an open implementation: 0.002 of the screen, about 2 pixels at 1080p. — [deepwiki on meshoptimizer](https://deepwiki.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/3.2-nanite-like-lod-system)
- `[S]` Speed of the software rasteriser relative to hardware: about 3x on average (see section 4). — [CuRast, arXiv 2604.21749](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.21749)

### Inferences
- At the confirmed 14.4 bytes per input triangle, a 200,000-triangle asset is about 2.9 MB and a 10-million-triangle asset about 144 MB on disk in Nanite's own compressed format. An engine that builds its assets at start-up and never writes them to disk cares about the in-memory figure instead, which was not confirmed (see Gaps).
- "5.6 bytes per Nanite triangle" and a per-input-triangle figure about twice as large are consistent with the DAG holding about 2T triangles in total (section 1).
- The 16 million cap means Nanite does not itself hold hundreds of millions of instances at once. Unreal, as I recall it, relies on streaming the world in pieces and on merged far proxies to stay under the cap (recalled; see section 6 Gaps).

### Gaps
- **Build time per million triangles: not found** (see section 1 for the two unread leads).
- **Streaming granularity: not confirmed.** RECALLED (medium to low, UE source): fixed-size pages of 128 KB for streamed data and a 32 KB root page per mesh that always stays resident so that something can always be drawn; clusters are packed into pages by level and spatial locality. → verify in the [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf), streaming slides.
- **The 2021 results table: recalled only, but its arithmetic is self-consistent.** RECALLED (high for the set as a whole): for the "Lumen in the Land of Nanite" demo the Deck lists 433 million input triangles, 882 million Nanite triangles, 25.90 GB raw, 7.67 GB in the memory format, 4.61 GB in the compressed disk format, "5.6 bytes per Nanite triangle", "11.4 bytes per input triangle" and "1M triangles = ~10.9MB on disk". Check: 4.61 GiB / 882 M = 5.6 bytes; 4.61 GiB / 433 M = 11.4 bytes; 882/433 = 2.04. The memory format works out at about 9.3 bytes per stored triangle, or 19 bytes per input triangle.
- **Conflict to resolve:** 11.4 bytes per input triangle (Deck, recalled, first demo) against 14.4 (documentation, confirmed, a different sample project); and 6.14 GB (livestream notes) against 4.61 GB (Deck, recalled, which also claims about 20 % improvement since Early Access). These are probably different content and dates, not contradictions.
- **Frame cost and triangles per frame: not confirmed.** RECALLED (medium): on PlayStation 5 at an average of about 1400p upsampled to 4K, culling plus rasterisation took about 2.5 ms and the material pass about 2 ms; a typical frame went from 896,322 instances before culling to 3,668 after, visited 184,828 hierarchy nodes, and drew 127,216 clusters in software against 1,650 in hardware. RECALLED (high): Epic's 2020 reveal stated over a billion source triangles per frame reduced to around 20 million drawn triangles.
- RECALLED (medium): the documentation compares a 1.5-million-triangle mesh stored as a classic static mesh with four LODs (148.95 MB) against its Nanite form (19.64 MB).

## 6. The far distance for whole instances

### Takeaway
The DAG stops at one root cluster of up to 128 triangles per instance, so it does not by itself take an object "down to a pixel", and every instance still costs a culling test. Nanite's answers are outside the DAG: small precomputed imposters per mesh (confirmed only by a forum post), merged far proxies (recalled; confirmed this session only for ray tracing), and since 5.7 a voxel representation for distant foliage. How Nanite treats an instance smaller than a pixel could not be confirmed.

### Cited Findings
- `[S-weak]` Built-in imposters: Nanite pre-renders 12×12-pixel imposter images from 144 view directions and uses the closest one when an object's on-screen bounds fall under about 12 pixels; this costs memory and can pop. — [Unreal Engine forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/nanite-material-switching-based-on-distance-lods-replacement/1247397/5)
- `[P]` Instance count scales linearly and was judged acceptable only "within the limit of the scale of the level you typically want loaded around you". — [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- `[P]` Hard cap of 16 million instances per scene. — [Docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-US/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine)
- `[P]` Karis describes hierarchical LOD as a hierarchy of triangle-mesh chunks with a view-dependent cut drawn each frame, and says he likes the idea but not its crack solution. — [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- `[P]` Since 5.7 (experimental): Nanite Voxels handle distant foliage geometry; Nanite Assemblies treat instanced parts of a plant as one unit and cut storage and memory. — [Unreal Engine 5.7 release notes](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/unreal-engine-5-7-release-notes), [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/unreal-engine-5-7-is-now-availabe)
- `[S]` For ray tracing, Epic uses simplified proxy meshes, aggressive instance culling, aggregate representations, and a far-field representation that merges instances to cut triangle count. — [MegaLights, SIGGRAPH 2025 Advances](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2025/content/MegaLights_Stochastic_Direct_Lighting_2025.pdf)
- `[P]` Every Nanite mesh also carries a coarse fallback mesh, used for collision, ray tracing, light baking and platforms without Nanite. — [Docs: Nanite technical details](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/nanite-technical-details)

### Inferences
- **The floor of the DAG is a cluster, not a pixel.** A distant instance costs one culling test plus up to 128 triangles however small it is on screen. With millions of visible small instances that floor dominates, which is why Nanite adds imposters and why Epic's far-distance tools all merge or replace instances rather than simplify them further.
- **An engine with hundreds of millions of instances needs an instance-level hierarchy that Nanite did not have in 2021**: something that culls or replaces whole groups of instances before any per-instance work. The confirmed sources show Epic's direction (merged proxies, assemblies, voxels) but not a mechanism inside the cluster DAG.

### Gaps
- **Imposter details: confirmed only by a forum post.** RECALLED (high for the first two, medium for the rest): the Deck's slide lists 12 × 12 view directions in an atlas, 12 × 12 pixels per direction, an octahedral mapping of direction to atlas position, an 8-bit depth and 8-bit triangle ID per pixel, "40.5KB per mesh always resident" (144 × 144 × 2 bytes = 41,472 bytes, which matches), a short ray march to correct parallax between directions, drawing directly from the instance-culling pass, and the remark that Epic "would like to replace this with something better". → verify in the [Deck](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf), slides on tiny instances.
- **Whether imposters are still generated by default in current engine versions: unknown.**
- **What Nanite does with an instance smaller than a pixel: not found.** Whether it is culled by size, drawn as an imposter, or drawn as a root cluster by default was not confirmed.
- **Merged far proxies (World Partition HLOD) with Nanite: not searched** before the budget ran out. RECALLED (medium to high): Unreal's HLOD layers can build merged or simplified proxy meshes for distant cells, and those proxies can themselves be Nanite meshes.
- RECALLED (medium): from 5.4 the engine has an instance-culling hierarchy ("scene culling") so that instance culling no longer tests every instance; not confirmed.

### Takeaway
Confirmed this session: Epic's documentation now lists skeletal, spline and instanced components as supported, and 5.7 (November 2025) shipped an experimental foliage system made of assemblies, skinning and voxels, which is Epic's answer to the geometry the original algorithm handles worst. Recalled but not confirmed: Nanite began in 2021 for rigid, opaque static meshes only, and Epic's documentation names "aggregate" geometry (foliage, grass, hair) as its weak case. ### Inferences
- **What the cluster-DAG algorithm does not solve** (my reasoning from sections 1 to 6; each point says what is confirmed):
  1. **Aggregates.** Thousands of unconnected cards or leaves cannot be simplified by edge collapse with locked borders, and they defeat occlusion culling. Epic's remedy four years later is a different representation (voxels) plus instancing inside the asset (assemblies).
  2. **Whole-instance count.** The algorithm is per instance and bottoms out at one cluster (section 6).
  3. **Deformation.** Errors and spheres are computed for the rest shape, so skinning and vertex offsets need conservative bounds. Confirmed: in 5.7 Nanite Skinning is a separate and still experimental feature, and world-position offset has weak Nanite support. The earlier history is recalled only (table below).
  4. **Transparency and alpha-cut surfaces.** A visibility buffer holds one surface per pixel, and an alpha-cut card is not the shape its triangles describe, so geometric error says nothing about its appearance.
  5. **Material and shading detail.** Simplification preserves geometry and attributes; it does not turn lost geometric roughness into shading.
  6. **Popping above the pixel threshold** (section 3).
  7. **Build cost.** It is an offline import step in Unreal (section 1).
- For an asset set of closed rocks, flat-faced buildings, tube-and-card trees and skinned creatures, the algorithm as published fits the first two kinds and needs the later additions (or a different technique) for the last two.

## 8. Lineage: the earlier research

### Takeaway
Confirmed this session: Karis's own account of the path (geometry images, then a geomorphing chunk hierarchy after Progressive Buffers, then voxels, then triangle clusters), his rejection of per-vertex schemes, and what Progressive Buffers contributed. Recalled but not re-read: the level-of-detail core is not new. Selecting a view-dependent cut by projected error comes from view-dependent progressive meshes (1996 to 1997); making the cluster rather than the vertex the unit of selection comes from Quick-VDR and Adaptive TetraPuzzles (2004); the DAG whose borders alternate between levels comes from Batched Multi-Triangulation (2005) and is explained at length in Ponchio's thesis (2008). The paper-by-paper contributions under Gaps need checking against the papers.

### Cited Findings
- `[P]` Karis's path as he tells it: from virtual textures to "virtual geometry images" (inspired by Catmull-Clark-style quad meshes; this first step is from a digest that did not name which Karis deck); then a hierarchical-LOD scheme of mesh chunks in a sparse octree, with Progressive Buffers geomorphing to avoid cracks; then voxels; then irregular triangle clusters, starting from the question of what neighbouring clusters minimally need to know of each other to guarantee no cracks. — [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- `[P]` The keynote was given at the in-person reception of High-Performance Graphics 2022 (Vancouver, 7 August 2022, co-located with SIGGRAPH; the conference itself was virtual that year). — [HPG 2022 programme](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/2022/program/), [keynote page](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/2022/in-person-reception-keynote/)
- `[P]` View-dependent progressive meshes were considered and set aside because "making decisions per vertex is excessive". — [HPG deck](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- `[P]` Progressive Buffers (Sander & Mitchell): static "GPU-friendly" vertex and index buffers; the level of a cluster is chosen from the distance of its bounding sphere to the camera; geometry is geomorphed in the vertex shader with per-vertex weights, which "completely prevents LOD pops and boundary cracks". — [SIGGRAPH 2006 course chapter](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2006/Chapter1-Out-of-Core_Rendering_of_Large_Meshes_with_Progressive_Buffers.pdf)
- `[P]` View-dependent progressive meshes as described in a US patent on run-time geomorphs (to my knowledge Hoppe's, for Microsoft; the digest did not show the inventor): a vertex hierarchy whose roots are the base-mesh vertices and whose leaves are the fully detailed mesh, with geomorphs created at run time so that transitions do not visibly snap. — [US patent 6,426,750, "Run-time geomorphs"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6426750)
- `[S]` The rendering side has its own lineage: Aaltonen says the clustered rendering and two-pass occlusion culling he presented years earlier have become a de-facto standard; to my knowledge that earlier talk is "GPU-Driven Rendering Pipelines" (SIGGRAPH 2015). — [Aaltonen 2023](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2023/AaltonenHypeHypeAdvances2023.pdf), [Haar & Aaltonen 2015](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/aaltonenhaar_siggraph2015_combined_final_footer_220dpi.pdf)
- `[S]` Descendants that show the algorithm is reproducible outside Unreal: Tencent's mobile NanoMesh (2024), the meshoptimizer cluster-LOD builder, and several hobby and student renderers. — [Tencent NanoMesh](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2024/content/Cao-NanoMesh/AdavanceRealtimeRendering_NanoMesh0810.pdf), [zeux/meshoptimizer discussion #750](https://github.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/discussions/750), [jglrxavpok](https://jglrxavpok.github.io/2024/04/02/recreating-nanite-runtime-lod-selection.html), [Vulcanite](https://github.com/bdwhst/Vulcanite)

### Inferences
- **Mapping of ideas** (first column confirmed where marked, otherwise from the recalled paper summaries in Gaps):

  | Idea in Nanite | Where it comes from |
  |---|---|
  | Simplify by edge collapse, driven by an error measure | Progressive Meshes (Hoppe 1996) |
  | Draw a view-dependent cut through a hierarchy, chosen by error projected to the screen | View-dependent progressive meshes (Hoppe 1997); Luebke & Erikson 1997 |
  | Decide per cluster, not per vertex; cull and stream per cluster | Quick-VDR (2004); Adaptive TetraPuzzles (2004). Karis's rejection of per-vertex decisions is confirmed |
  | Merge neighbouring patches, simplify with the outer border locked, so that independently chosen patches join without cracks | Adaptive TetraPuzzles (2004) |
  | Re-partition at every level so that locked borders alternate; the result is a DAG, not a tree | Batched Multi-Triangulation (2005); explained at length in Ponchio's thesis (2008) |
  | Errors and bounding spheres made monotonic so that a simple local test finds the cut | TetraPuzzles and Batched Multi-Triangulation |
  | Static GPU-friendly buffers per cluster, loaded out of core | Progressive Buffers (2005); the static buffers and the out-of-core use are confirmed from the course chapter and its title |
  | Geomorphing between levels | Present in Progressive Meshes and Progressive Buffers; **deliberately not taken** by Nanite (confirmed for Progressive Buffers) |

- **What Nanite added** on top of the lineage, as far as the confirmed sources show: very small clusters (128 triangles instead of thousands), graph partitioning for both clusters and groups, evaluation of the cut entirely in parallel on the GPU, and the pixel-scale rendering machinery of section 4.

### Gaps
- **Which of these papers the Nanite talks themselves cite was not confirmed**: two searches for Quick-VDR, Batched Multi-Triangulation, TetraPuzzles and Ponchio in the two decks returned nothing before the budget ran out. RECALLED (medium): the 2021 Deck credits Quick-VDR and Batched Multi-Triangulation for the cluster hierarchy with grouped, alternating borders, and recommends Ponchio's thesis as the clearest explanation; Progressive Buffers appears in the HPG deck (confirmed).
- **Paper-by-paper contributions: all recalled, none re-read.** Links are the authors' pages as remembered and were not opened.
  - RECALLED (high): **Hoppe, "Progressive Meshes", SIGGRAPH 1996.** Stores a mesh as a coarse base mesh plus a sequence of vertex splits, the inverse of edge collapses; gives continuous resolution, progressive transmission, compression, selective refinement and geomorphs (smooth interpolation between any two resolutions). → [hhoppe.com/proj/pm](https://hhoppe.com/proj/pm/)
  - RECALLED (high): **Hoppe, "View-Dependent Refinement of Progressive Meshes", SIGGRAPH 1997.** Turns the split sequence into a vertex hierarchy and refines it selectively each frame by view frustum, surface orientation and screen-space geometric error; exploits frame-to-frame coherence; geomorphs at run time. A split is only legal when certain neighbouring faces exist, which creates dependencies between vertices. → [hhoppe.com/proj/vdrpm](https://hhoppe.com/proj/vdrpm/)
  - RECALLED (high): **Luebke & Erikson, "View-Dependent Simplification of Arbitrary Polygonal Environments", SIGGRAPH 1997.** A vertex tree built by spatial clustering, folded and unfolded each frame against a screen-space error threshold, with silhouette preservation and a triangle budget; works on arbitrary polygon soup without requiring clean topology. → [ACM DL, doi 10.1145/258734.258847](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/258734.258847)
  - RECALLED (medium to high): **Yoon, Salomon, Gayle, Manocha, "Quick-VDR: Interactive View-Dependent Rendering of Massive Models", IEEE Visualization 2004.** A clustered hierarchy of progressive meshes: a cluster hierarchy for coarse refinement with a progressive mesh inside each cluster for fine refinement; dependencies are tracked between clusters instead of between vertices; out-of-core construction; occlusion culling per cluster; models of hundreds of millions of triangles. It is a tree of clusters, the structure whose persistent borders Karis criticises. → [gamma.cs.unc.edu/QVDR](http://gamma.cs.unc.edu/QVDR/)
  - RECALLED (high): **Cignoni, Ganovelli, Gobbetti, Marton, Ponchio, Scopigno, "Adaptive TetraPuzzles", SIGGRAPH 2004.** Space is partitioned by a hierarchy of tetrahedra made by longest-edge bisection; each tetrahedron holds a precomputed patch of a few thousand triangles, stored as a GPU-friendly batch; patches are built bottom-up by merging and simplifying with constraints on the borders so that any consistent selection joins without cracks; errors and bounding volumes are made nested so that a simple test finds the cut. → [vcg.isti.cnr.it/Publications/2004/CGGMPS04](https://vcg.isti.cnr.it/Publications/2004/CGGMPS04/)
  - RECALLED (high): **Cignoni et al., "Batched Multi Triangulation", IEEE Visualization 2005.** Generalises TetraPuzzles with the Multi-Triangulation framework, a DAG of local mesh replacements, using patches of thousands of triangles as the DAG's nodes; shows that any sequence of partitions whose borders differ from level to level (their "V-partition") yields a crack-free DAG when each region is simplified with its border locked. This is the same construction as Nanite's regrouping. → [vcg.isti.cnr.it/Publications/2005/CGGMPS05](https://vcg.isti.cnr.it/Publications/2005/CGGMPS05/)
  - RECALLED (high for title, medium for content): **Ponchio, "Multiresolution structures for interactive visualization of very large 3D datasets", PhD thesis, Clausthal University of Technology, 2008.** Consolidates the terrain work, TetraPuzzles and Batched Multi-Triangulation into one framework and explains the alternating-partition DAG and the monotonic error and bounds that allow independent per-node tests. → [vcg.isti.cnr.it/~ponchio](https://vcg.isti.cnr.it/~ponchio/download/ponchio_phd.pdf)
  - RECALLED (medium to high): **Sander & Mitchell, "Progressive Buffers: View-dependent Geometry and Texture LOD Rendering", Symposium on Geometry Processing 2005.** Beyond what is confirmed above: each vertex stores its position in the next coarser level; the blend weight is a function of the vertex's own distance to the camera, which is why vertices shared by two clusters move identically and no stitching is needed; the same structure streams geometry and texture out of core. Its requirement that the buffers for a whole distance band be loaded is exactly what Karis objects to (confirmed).
- **Not covered at all:** Xia & Varshney 1996 (merge trees), the Multi-Triangulation papers of De Floriani, Magillo and Puppo, and the terrain predecessors (BDAM) that the Italian group built on.
- **Unread lead:** a SIGGRAPH 2026 Advances talk from Roblox on scaling user-generated 3D worlds ("SLIM") surfaced by title only; whether it uses a cluster DAG is unknown. — [slides](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2026/content/SMAK_SIG26_SLIM-Scaling_User-Generated_3D_Worlds_on_Roblox_8_Aug_2026.pdf)
