## 1. REYES "bound, split, dice" micropolygon rendering and its real-time descendants (GPU tessellation, Nanite tessellation and displacement, NVIDIA Micro-Mesh)

### Takeaway
Reyes (1987) states the principle in its purest form: never store a level of detail, instead bound each primitive, split it until it is "diceable", and dice it into a grid of micropolygons about half a pixel wide for the current view. Its real-time descendants keep the rule and differ in where dicing runs: fixed-function tessellators (legacy, absent from WebGPU and judged unlikely ever to arrive), software/compute dicing inside Nanite (UE 5.3 onward, still described as experimental in the documentation that search surfaced), and compute-shader tessellation (John Hable, SIGGRAPH 2026, shown running in WebGPU); NVIDIA Micro-Mesh (2022 to 2023) is superseded and archived. Every crack-free variant uses one rule: the subdivision of a shared edge must be computed only from data that both neighbours see identically.

### Cited Findings

#### Reyes (Cook, Carpenter, Catmull 1987)
- "The Reyes Image Rendering Architecture" by Robert L. Cook, Loren Carpenter and Edwin Catmull appeared in the SIGGRAPH '87 proceedings, pages 95 to 102; the architecture was developed in the mid-1980s by Carpenter and Cook at Lucasfilm's Computer Graphics Research Group, now Pixar — [Wikipedia: Reyes rendering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reyes_rendering); [Pixar online library](https://graphics.pixar.com/library/Reyes)
- The design goal was fast, high-quality rendering of complex images, with "fast" defined as "being able to compute a feature-length film in approximately a year" — [paper copy, University of Utah](https://my.eng.utah.edu/~cs5610/handouts/reyes.pdf)
- Core principle: "All objects are reduced to common world-space geometric entities called micropolygons, and all of the shading and visibility calculations operate on these micropolygons." — [paper copy](https://my.eng.utah.edu/~cs5610/handouts/reyes.pdf)
- Dicing rate: "Primitives are diced so that micropolygons are approximately half a pixel on a side in screen space", and "Micropolygons are processed in large grids which allow computations to be vectorized." — [paper copy](https://my.eng.utah.edu/~cs5610/handouts/reyes.pdf)
- Split or dice: a per-primitive "diceable" routine decides whether a primitive can be diced directly or must be split first; it answers "not diceable" when dicing would yield too many micropolygons or a wide range of micropolygon sizes (the paper's example is a sphere: small ones pass, large ones are split) — [ResearchGate copy of the paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220721047_The_Reyes_Image_Rendering_Architecture)
- After dicing and shading, the grid is busted into individual micropolygons, each bounded and checked for visibility, then hidden and sampled to produce the image — [Wikipedia: Reyes rendering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reyes_rendering)
- The paper's "locality" principle (geometric and texture locality, to limit paging) covers procedural models, noting that their expanded form should not be kept longer than necessary — [Scribd copy of the paper](https://www.scribd.com/document/635213960/The-Reyes-Image-Rendering-Architecture)
- Later practice relaxed the rate: the original implementation kept quads no larger than half a pixel, while later Reyes-style renderers often used about one pixel because shaders antialias themselves — [Owens et al., Graphics Hardware 2002](http://cva.stanford.edu/publications/2002/owens-reyes-opengl-gh02.pdf)
- Superseded in its original home: Pixar's RenderMan implemented Reyes, but the algorithm "was deprecated as of 2016 and removed as of RenderMan 21" — [Wikipedia: Reyes rendering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reyes_rendering)

#### Cracks in split-and-dice, and the crack-free rule
- DiagSplit (Fisher et al., SIGGRAPH Asia 2009) is "a parallel algorithm for adaptively tessellating displaced parametric surfaces into high-quality, crack-free micropolygon meshes"; it allows splits along non-isoparametric directions and a unique tessellation factor per subpatch edge, and "edge tessellation factors are computed using only information local to subpatch edges", so all subpatches can be processed independently "without introducing T-junctions or mesh cracks and without incurring the tessellation overhead of binary dicing" — [DiagSplit project page, Stanford](http://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/diagsplit/); [paper PDF](http://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/diagsplit/diagSplitPaper.pdf)
- A later GPU-Reyes thesis describes DiagSplit as avoiding surface cracks by combining the scheme used in hardware tessellation with Reyes — [Weber, "Micropolygon Rendering on the GPU", TU Wien 2015](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2015/WEBER-2015-PRA1/WEBER-2015-PRA1-thesis.pdf)

#### Hardware (fixed-function) tessellation with screen-space adaptive factors
- The OpenGL tessellation specification names the cause of cracks and the rule that prevents them: "the most likely cause of cracking is due to shared patch edges being subdivided differently", so shaders "must be constructed so that outer tessellation level corresponding to the shared edge must be exactly the same in both patches" — [ARB_tessellation_shader specification](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL/extensions/ARB/ARB_tessellation_shader.txt)
- With `fractional_even_spacing`, each level is clamped to [2, max] and rounded up to the nearest even integer to give the effective level — [Khronos OpenGL Wiki: Tessellation](https://www.khronos.org/opengl/wiki/Tessellation). An older article gives the clamp range as [1, 63]; the specification should be trusted — [Geeks3D](https://www.geeks3d.com/20130128/know-your-opengl-tessellation-spacing-modes/)
- Floating-point levels with `fractional_even_spacing` or `fractional_odd_spacing` "allow smooth transition between different levels of tessellation (useful for LOD algorithms)", avoiding the popping that integer levels cause — [Geeks3D](https://www.geeks3d.com/20130128/know-your-opengl-tessellation-spacing-modes/); [explainer on fractional spacing](https://salivity.github.io/glsl/article/how-fractional-even-spacing-stops-tessellation-popping)
- Crack-free evaluation also depends on bit-exact arithmetic: the OpenGL ES extension relies on `precise` and `fma` from the gpu_shader5 extensions — [OES_tessellation_shader specification](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenGL/extensions/OES/OES_tessellation_shader.txt)
- Setting the factor of a shared edge to the same value in both patches guarantees crack-free tessellation; power-of-two factors are a workable but restrictive way to line edges up; a variant keeps positions and normals watertight so displaced surfaces also render without cracks — [Nießner, "Rendering Subdivision Surfaces using Hardware Tessellation" (thesis, 2013)](http://www.graphics.stanford.edu/~niessner/papers/2013/1thesis/niessner2013thesis.pdf)
- For displacement, neighbouring patches of different resolution need consistent data along their boundaries, and displacement mip levels are selected by tessellation density to avoid under-sampling — [Nießner and Loop, "Analytic Displacement Mapping using Hardware Tessellation"](https://niessnerlab.org/papers/2013/3analytic/niessner2013analytic.pdf)
- Screen-space adaptivity: factors can be driven by projected screen size or by curvature, while neighbouring patches must still match their outer factors — [AMD GPUOpen, "GPU view-adaptive crack-free subdivision of Bézier surfaces"](https://gpuopen.com/learn/gpu-view-adaptive-subdivision/)
- A known limit of a common terrain recipe: NVIDIA's DirectX 11 terrain tessellation whitepaper says its crack-free method does not work for patches of differing sizes — [Cantlay, NVIDIA whitepaper](https://developer.download.nvidia.cn/assets/gamedev/files/sdk/11/TerrainTessellation_WhitePaper.pdf)
- The survey literature describes "FracSplit", which handles fractional edge factors by giving the shared edge the larger factor and interpolating the split across the patch — [Nießner et al., "Real-time Rendering Techniques with Hardware Tessellation"](https://techmatt.github.io/pdfs/realtimeRendering.pdf)
- Status, engines: forum users report that hardware tessellation was deprecated in Unreal Engine 4.26 and completely removed in 5.0 (community reports, not an official Epic statement) — [Unreal forums: "Tessellation deprecated?"](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/tessellation-deprecated/154558)
- Status, WebGPU: a January 2026 comment on the wgpu tracking issue says the consensus is that tessellation shaders "will most likely not happen", because Metal's tessellation API is too different from Direct3D 12 and Vulkan and because tessellation shaders matter less now that mesh shaders exist (a maintainer's view in an issue thread, not a W3C decision) — [gfx-rs/wgpu issue 222](https://github.com/gfx-rs/wgpu/issues/222)
- Mesh shaders are tracked for WebGPU but are not part of it — [gpuweb issue 3015](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/issues/3015); [Linebender wiki: Mesh shaders](https://linebender.org/wiki/gpu/mesh-shaders/)

#### Nanite tessellation and displacement (Unreal Engine 5.3 onward)
- Epic describes Nanite Tessellation as "dynamic programmable displacement that allows for Nanite meshes to be modified at runtime using a displacement map or procedural material"; a separate offline adaptive tessellator in the Static Mesh Editor bakes displacement into a Nanite mesh — [Epic documentation: Nanite Virtualized Geometry](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine)
- It is enabled with the console variables `r.Nanite.AllowTessellation=1` and `r.Nanite.Tessellation=1` plus an "Enable Tessellation" checkbox on the asset; a community guide adds that the Nanite Displaced Mesh plugin must be enabled — [Epic documentation](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine); [Unreal Stack guide](https://unrealstack.com/how-to-use-ue5-nanite-tessellation/)
- Forum guidance relayed from Epic is to keep displacement small: a crater pressed into a landscape is a bad fit because collision would not follow the displaced surface — [Unreal forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/question-about-nanite-tessellation-being-core-to-asset-creation-workflow/2641356)
- Status is reported inconsistently: one guide says it was introduced as experimental in 5.3 and reached Beta in 5.4 — [Unreal Stack guide](https://unrealstack.com/how-to-use-ue5-nanite-tessellation/); a forum post notes that the official documentation still called it experimental in 5.6 — [Unreal forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/question-about-nanite-tessellation-being-core-to-asset-creation-workflow/2641356)
- The lead author's own write-up began in February 2026: Brian Karis announced "I'm finally writing up how Nanite Tessellation works", describing a system for dynamically tessellating meshes and displacing them, with the displacement coming from a shader graph authored in the material editor — [Graphic Rants: "Nanite Tessellation"](http://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2026/02/nanite-tessellation.html); [announcement on X](https://x.com/BrianKaris/status/2018155984678736293)
- Nanite plus Reyes: cluster triangles are treated as patches with a displacement function and, depending on their size, may need additional tessellation before rasterising, so a single mesh can see both simplification (base Nanite) and amplification (tessellation); patches whose tessellation factors all fall within a maximum dice factor go to a dice queue and the rest go to a split queue, where they are split into child subpatches according to split factors and a tessellation table; "unlike clusters, patches don't have a hardware rasterization path" — [Graphic Rants: "Nanite + Reyes"](https://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2026/02/nanite-reyes.html)
- Constraints stated in the same post: displaced bounds are unknown until the displacement runs, so the user must specify a scalar maximum displacement range; patch splitting runs in one shader for all materials, so that logic is fixed-function, with "no user programmable normals, no programmable dicing rate"; sharp discontinuities in the displacement function are the worst case because a diced triangle then stretches from minimum to maximum displacement; currently only materials with displacement mapping enable tessellation — [Graphic Rants: "Nanite + Reyes"](https://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2026/02/nanite-reyes.html)
- How it tessellates: tessellation factors index a precomputed "Tessellation Table" whose entries (patterns) are stored like small meshes with a vertex and index buffer; factors are treated as patch edge lengths and a pattern aims for triangles with roughly unit-length edges; "it is important that we don't create cracks between neighboring patches or subpatches", and the quantisation of factors "needs to be symmetric"; uniform dicing with the table yields 69% of the triangles of Direct3D-style uniform topology — [Graphic Rants: "How to tessellate"](http://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2026/02/how-to-tessellate.html)
- The console variable `r.Nanite.DicingRate` sets how large in screen space diced triangles should be; material displacement fade defaults start at 4 and end at 1 — [Unreal forums: Nanite tessellation dicing](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/nanite-tessellation-dicing-disable-distance/2623536)
- Known weaknesses: a 2026 SIGGRAPH course slide lists Nanite tessellation as using integer tessellation factors and nested splits and notes that the pattern pops when factors change — [Hable, "Adaptive Tessellation and Subdivision" slides](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2026/content/Adaptive%20Tessellation%20and%20Subdivision_7.pdf); an Epic issue reports floating "ghost" terrain in UE 5.7.3 when displacement is scaled in the material graph, apparently because bounds assume the full 0 to 1 range — [Epic issue UE-370671](https://issues.unrealengine.com/issue/UE-370671)
- Engine timeline: Unreal Engine 5.8 was released on 17 June 2026 and Epic calls it "the last planned major Unreal Engine 5 release on our roadmap as we ramp up work on UE6" — [Epic: Unreal Engine 5.8 is now available](https://www.unrealengine.com/news/unreal-engine-5-8-is-now-available); a user in the release thread reports Nanite tessellation breaking up the new experimental Mesh Terrain with distance — [Unreal forums: 5.8 released](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/unreal-engine-5-8-released/2729274)

#### Compute-shader tessellation without tessellation stages (2026)
- John Hable (Meta) presented "Adaptive Tessellation and Subdivision" in the SIGGRAPH 2026 Advances in Real-Time Rendering course (21 July 2026): tessellation and subdivision with compute shaders, a "clamped parallelogram" tessellation pattern, "a full compute-based pipeline to tessellate meshes with screen-adaptive tessellation while welding seams", extended to Catmull-Clark subdivision with semi-sharp creases — [SIGGRAPH 2026 Advances course page](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2026/index.html)
- The pattern keeps floating-point factors like Direct3D 11 tessellation but replaces the pattern with a straighter, more stable one; the post calls it a continuous, popless triangle algorithm that can transition all the way from a single triangle — [Filmic Worlds: "Triangle Tessellation with Clamped Parallelograms" (May 2026)](https://filmicworlds.com/blog/compute-tessellation-with-clamped-parallelograms/)
- The mesh-level follow-up includes a live WebGPU tessellation viewer, uses a precomputed lookup table, tessellates into large flat buffers, handles buffer overflow by zeroing tessellation factors, and prefers angular arc length from the camera (converted to a rough pixel proxy) over post-projection screen-space length, which the author finds "a bit finicky and unstable" — [Filmic Worlds: "Adaptive Compute Tessellation" (21 May 2026)](https://filmicworlds.com/blog/adaptive-compute-tessellation/)
- A June 2026 post extends this to Catmull-Clark subdivision — [Filmic Worlds: "Adaptive Catmull-Clark Subdivision with Compute Tessellation"](https://filmicworlds.com/blog/adaptive-catmull-clark-subdivision-with-compute-tessellation/)

#### NVIDIA Micro-Mesh (2022 to 2023), superseded
- NVIDIA presented Micro-Mesh as a way to build highly detailed geometry that is compact and can be directly rasterised or ray traced in real time without conversion or expansion — [NVIDIA Micro-Mesh page](https://developer.nvidia.com/rtx/ray-tracing/micro-mesh); it was introduced with the Ada architecture, which compresses displacement magnitudes into maps, one per base triangle — [NVIDIA Ada GPU architecture whitepaper](https://images.nvidia.com/aem-dam/Solutions/geforce/ada/nvidia-ada-gpu-architecture.pdf)
- Each base triangle has its own power-of-two subdivision level, but "to ensure watertightness they are allowed to only differ by one subdivision level"; edge flags mark an edge whose neighbour is one level lower, telling the finer triangle to decimate that edge to avoid cracks — [Displacement MicroMap Toolkit: data structures](https://github.com/NVIDIAGameWorks/Displacement-MicroMap-Toolkit/blob/main/docs/data_structures.md)
- Limits: hardware ray tracing supports up to subdivision level 5 (1,024 micro-triangles per base triangle); meshlets for rasterisation are capped at 64 micro-triangles and 45 vertices (level 3); triangle indices must be rebuilt per level and per edge-decimation permutation — [NVIDIA slides: Micro-Mesh Basics](https://developer.download.nvidia.com/ProGraphics/nvpro-samples/slides/Micro-Mesh_Basics.pdf); [Micro-Mesh Rasterization](https://developer.download.nvidia.com/ProGraphics/nvpro-samples/slides/Micro-Mesh_Rasterization.pdf)
- Superseded: the sample repository states "The NVIDIA RTX Mega Geometry technology supercedes displaced Micro-Meshes. This repository is archived as result." — [nvpro-samples/vk_displacement_micromaps](https://github.com/nvpro-samples/vk_displacement_micromaps); a sibling sample is archived and marked deprecated with the same notice — [nvpro-samples/vk_raytrace_displacement](https://github.com/nvpro-samples/vk_raytrace_displacement); a related NVIDIA library is archived with the note that "the vulkan extension VK_NV_displacement_micromap is no longer available" (read through a mirror of its README) — [NVIDIAGameWorks/heightmap_rtx](https://github.com/NVIDIAGameWorks/heightmap_rtx); NVIDIA's marketing page still promotes the SDK and appears stale — [NVIDIA Micro-Mesh page](https://developer.nvidia.com/rtx/ray-tracing/micro-mesh). One game-studio blog instead frames Micro-Mesh and Mega Geometry as separate stacks — [StraySpark blog](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/nvidia-rtx-mega-geometry-neural-rendering-ue5-foliage)
- The successor, RTX Mega Geometry (OptiX 9, 2025), builds cluster acceleration structures over small user-defined triangle clusters; for subdivision surfaces NVIDIA's recipe is to define clusters on the limit surface "at a density of about one triangle per pixel" and re-tessellate each frame — [NVIDIA developer blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/fast-ray-tracing-of-dynamic-scenes-using-nvidia-optix-9-and-nvidia-rtx-mega-geometry)

### Inferences
- The Reyes rule is the cleanest available statement of a single engine-wide rule: choose the generator's sampling density so that one output primitive covers a fixed number of pixels (half a pixel in 1987, about one pixel later). A set of fixed power-of-two resolutions per asset is a quantised dicing rate: adjacent levels differ by a factor of two in edge length, so the on-screen primitive size swings by a factor of two around the target unless levels are blended.
- All crack-free schemes found (edge-local factors in DiagSplit, equal outer levels in the OpenGL specification, symmetric quantisation in Nanite's tessellation table, the one-level rule with edge decimation flags in Micro-Mesh) are the same rule. A closed solid sampled at one resolution on all six faces of a cube-sphere satisfies it trivially, provided the vertices on the twelve cube edges are computed from bit-identical inputs. Cracks only become a problem if parts of one object use different resolutions.
- Fixed-function tessellation is a dead end for a WebGPU engine: it is not in the API, is judged unlikely to be added, and the largest engine that relied on it removed it. What replaced it runs in compute or in software (Nanite's dicing, Hable's compute tessellation, concurrent binary trees), and Hable's WebGPU viewer is direct evidence that screen-adaptive, seam-welded tessellation can run in a browser without tessellation or mesh shaders. I found no evidence of its cost on phones.
- Nanite tessellation shows that a production system needed a user-supplied displacement bound for culling and gave up programmable dicing rates; an engine that evaluates displacement on the GPU will need the same conservative bound per asset.
- Nanite's design (simplify below the authored density, dice above it) implies that for a surface defined by a formula there is no "authored density" at all: every level can come from dicing, and simplification is only needed for content that is not parametric.
- Nanite tessellation has been experimental or beta for about three years and still pops when integer factors change; continuous transitions are the hard part, not the dicing itself.
- Micro-Mesh is not usable in browsers and is superseded, but its data model (coarse base triangles, power-of-two barycentric displacement grids, neighbours within one level, edge decimation) is a compact description of nested regular levels over an arbitrary base mesh.

## 2. Quadtree and patch LOD for parametric surfaces: chunked LOD, geometry clipmaps, CDLOD, concurrent binary trees; application to cube-sphere solids with procedural displacement; feasibility on WebGPU

### Takeaway
The four methods form a clear progression in how they hide level changes: chunked LOD (2002) hides cracks with skirts and morphs whole chunks; geometry clipmaps (2004) and CDLOD (2009/2010) morph each vertex towards the next-coarser nested grid in the vertex shader, which makes transitions continuous and removes stitching; concurrent binary trees (2020, extended 2024) keep a conforming, crack-free triangulation entirely on the GPU with compute shaders and are the current state of the art at planetary scale. The vertex-shader morph of CDLOD and clipmaps needs nothing beyond what WebGPU offers, and working three.js and WebGPU examples exist, including cube-sphere planets whose heights are generated in compute shaders.

### Cited Findings

#### Chunked LOD (Ulrich 2002)
- "Rendering Massive Terrains using Chunked Level of Detail Control" was a SIGGRAPH 2002 course-notes contribution; it is a tile-based, out-of-core method built on a tree of static chunks — [TU Munich terrain rendering tutorial](https://www.cs.cit.tum.de/fileadmin/w00cfj/cg/Research/Tutorials/Terrain.pdf); [copy of the paper](https://masters.donntu.ru/2011/fknt/liseenko/library/article1.htm)
- Each node stores a maximum geometric error (larger towards the root, converging to zero at the leaves); at run time a screen-space error threshold selects which nodes to draw — [Leadwerks: large-scale terrain algorithms](https://www.leadwerks.com/community/blogs/entry/1163-large-scale-terrain-algorithms/); [TU Munich tutorial](https://www.cs.cit.tum.de/fileadmin/w00cfj/cg/Research/Tutorials/Terrain.pdf)
- Cracks between chunks of different levels are not removed but hidden: a skirt, "a strip of triangles that vertically extends around the perimeter of the chunk", sized by the maximum error bound; with skirts chunks need not be sewn together, they are simply placed side by side — [textbook chapter on chunked LODs](https://www.yaldex.com/game-programming/0131020099_ch14lev1sec5.html)
- Popping is handled by morphing vertices slowly to their final position, described in one tutorial as linear interpolation of the height of the centre vertex — [Leadwerks](https://www.leadwerks.com/community/blogs/entry/1163-large-scale-terrain-algorithms/); [TU Munich tutorial](https://www.cs.cit.tum.de/fileadmin/w00cfj/cg/Research/Tutorials/Terrain.pdf); developers discussing the paper note that it assigns one error value per tree level, halving at each level, which is a simplification rather than a guarantee — [GameDev.net thread](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/160989-chunked-lod-more-about-it/)

#### Geometry clipmaps (Losasso and Hoppe 2004; GPU version 2005)
- The terrain is cached "in a set of nested regular grids centered about the viewer", stored as vertex buffers and incrementally refilled as the viewpoint moves; level of detail is chosen in world space from viewer distance — [Hoppe: geometry clipmaps project page](https://hhoppe.com/proj/geomclipmap/); [paper](https://www.hhoppe.com/geomclipmap.pdf)
- The authors "create transition regions to smoothly blend between levels, and avoid T-junctions by stitching the level boundaries using zero-area triangles" — [paper](https://www.hhoppe.com/geomclipmap.pdf)
- In the GPU version the transition sits near the outer perimeter of each level, "whereby the geometry and textures are smoothly morphed to interpolate the next-coarser level", implemented in the vertex and pixel shaders: a blend parameter alpha is 0 except in a transition region of width n/10, where it ramps linearly to 1 at the outer boundary; the pixel shader blends the fine and coarse normal maps — [GPU Gems 2, chapter 2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry); [chapter PDF](https://hhoppe.com/gpugcm.pdf)
- Finer levels are predicted from coarser ones with a four-point subdivision interpolant (weights −1/16, 9/16, 9/16, −1/16), and then a decompression residual or synthesised fractal noise is added to obtain the actual elevation — [GPU Gems 2, chapter 2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)
- The related patent gives the transition width as w = min(n/10, min_width) — [US patent 7,436,405](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/7436405)

#### CDLOD (Strugar 2009/2010)
- "Continuous Distance-Dependent Level of Detail for Rendering Heightmaps" by Filip Strugar appeared in the Journal of Graphics, GPU, and Game Tools; sources give 2009 and 2010 as the year — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220494186_Continuous_Distance-Dependent_Level_of_Detail_for_Rendering_Heightmaps); [copy of the paper](https://aggrobird.com/files/cdlod_latest.pdf)
- The heightmap is organised into a quadtree of fixed-resolution grid blocks from which nodes of different levels are selected at run time; the level distribution is "a direct function of three-dimensional distance across all rendered vertices and is thus completely predictable" — [copy of the paper](https://aggrobird.com/files/cdlod_latest.pdf)
- The transition "completely transforms the mesh of a higher level into the lower detailed one" before the swap, by a smooth continuous morph, so no stitching meshes are needed — [copy of the paper](https://aggrobird.com/files/cdlod_latest.pdf)
- Unlike Ulrich's chunk-wide morph, each vertex is morphed individually from its own distance metric; the morph value runs from 0 (no morph, high-detail mesh) to 1 (full morph, a quarter of the triangles) and is applied in the vertex shader, which needs the grid dimension, the vertex position within the grid and the level; morphing starts in roughly the last 30 to 50 percent of a level's range; neighbouring nodes may differ by at most one level (tutorial and thesis write-ups of the method; which page carries which statement was not pinned) — [CDLOD terrain tutorial](https://svnte.se/cdlod-terrain); [Bournemouth MSc thesis](https://nccastaff.bournemouth.ac.uk/jmacey/MastersProject/MSc13/21/Procedural_Terrain_Generator.pdf)
- Applied to a sphere: an Android application "renders a planet using the CDLOD terrain technique with OpenGL ES 3.0" — [sduenasg/terrain-sandbox](https://github.com/sduenasg/terrain-sandbox)

#### Concurrent binary trees (Dupuy 2020; Benyoub and Dupuy 2024)
- A concurrent binary tree is "a binary heap, i.e., a 1D array, that explicitly stores the sum-reduction tree of a bitfield"; "threads can safely split and/or remove nodes concurrently via simple bitwise operations over the bitfield"; the paper applies it to longest-edge bisection and shows "a crack-free, 54×54 km terrain produced and rendered entirely on the GPU using the Unity game engine" — [Dupuy, Proc. ACM CGIT 2020](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3406186); [author's PDF](https://onrendering.com/data/papers/cbt/ConcurrentBinaryTrees.pdf)
- Search summaries of the paper report under five milliseconds per frame at full HD on an NVIDIA RTX 2080 for that terrain (the figure was returned by search without a pinned page; the records searched were the ACM entry and its mirrors) — [ACM Digital Library record](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3406186); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342626276_Concurrent_Binary_Trees_with_application_to_longest_edge_bisection)
- The reference code is GLSL: "the terrain geometry is computed and updated in parallel on the GPU using GLSL shaders" — [jdupuy/LongestEdgeBisection2D](https://github.com/jdupuy/LongestEdgeBisection2D); [jdupuy/libleb](https://github.com/jdupuy/libleb)
- "Concurrent Binary Trees for Large-Scale Game Components" (Anis Benyoub and Jonathan Dupuy, Intel; High-Performance Graphics 2024; arXiv 2407.02215) extends the method to arbitrary polygon meshes: each input halfedge is mapped to a triangular subdivision primitive called a bisector, and the tree is used "as a memory pool manager rather than an implicit encoding of the triangulation", so bisectors can be allocated and released concurrently in shared GPU memory — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.02215); [Intel developer article](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/cbt-for-large-scale-game-components.html)
- "By decoupling the CBT's capacity from the maximum subdivision depth, detailed and large-scale adaptive tessellations can be managed"; the authors stress that it uses a single representation at all viewing scales, avoiding the hybrid representations earlier methods needed — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.02215); [SIGGRAPH 2024 Advances slides](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2024/content/Intel/large_scale_cbt_slides_siggraph_advances_2024.pdf)
- Secondary summaries report that the triangulation evaluates in under 0.2 ms on console-level hardware, that centimetre-level detail runs in real time on mid-range GPUs, and that the method was validated on an Earth-sized water system and a Moon terrain built from NASA elevation data (secondary sites; which page carries which figure was not pinned, so check the paper) — [summary at Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2407.02215); [80 Level coverage](https://80.lv/articles/intel-researchers-presented-a-new-large-world-tessellation-approach); [project page](https://ggx-research.github.io/publication/2024/07/27/publication-cbt.html)
- A precursor that also avoids tessellation stages: "Adaptive GPU Tessellation with Compute Shaders" stores subdivision keys (a 64-bit value emulated with two 32-bit integers, allowing 63 levels) in a buffer; a compute pass updates the tree, a copy pass fills the indirect draw arguments, and the vertex shader decodes each key; the associated thesis argues that a parallel quadtree on the GPU yields crack-free surfaces without CPU preprocessing — [jadkhoury/TessellationDemo](https://github.com/jadkhoury/TessellationDemo); [Khoury, master's thesis](https://jadkhoury.github.io/files/MasterThesisFinal.pdf)

#### Closed solids as a cube-sphere with procedural displacement (planet renderers)
- Proland (Bruneton, INRIA) represents a planet as six terrain quadtrees, one per cube face, dynamically subdivided from the viewer position; new quads are generated when needed by GPU "producers" and cached on the GPU in a tile storage — [Proland documentation](http://proland.inrialpes.fr/doc/proland-4.0/core/html/index.html); [Eric Bruneton's page](https://evasion.inrialpes.fr/Membres/Eric.Bruneton/)
- A WebGPU project uses "cube-sphere planet geometry with GPU quadtree LOD" with a per-frame feedback buffer, and generates heightfields, splat maps and normals in WGSL compute shaders, with a noise library of fractional Brownian motion, ridged and domain-warped terms (third-party hobby project; quality not assessed) — [AI-Three-D/spherecraft](https://github.com/AI-Three-D/spherecraft)
- An OpenGL planet renderer runs the height pass and a shading pass as compute shaders, with patches queued through an asynchronous scheduler — [AcKeskin/procedural-planets](https://github.com/AcKeskin/procedural-planets)
- A Godot chunked-LOD planet uses a spherified-cube mapping instead of naive normalisation to avoid vertex bunching near the corners, keeps the chunk meshes flat and lets the shader add the noise displacement; chunks split near the camera and merge far away — [cuberact/godot-cuberact-planet-chunked-lod](https://github.com/cuberact/godot-cuberact-planet-chunked-lod)
- Skirts remain the common amateur crack fix: one walkthrough adds an extra row of edge vertices per chunk as a skirt — [procedural planet walkthrough](https://tigerabrodi.blog/building-a-procedural-planet-from-scratch-the-full-pipeline); a design specification warns that skirts only extend downward and cannot close upward gaps, recommending patch overlap first and skirts as backup — [Galaxy terrain LOD specification](https://erikevenson.github.io/galaxy/specs/mechanics/terrain-lod.html)

#### Feasibility on WebGPU (no tessellation or mesh shaders)
- WebGPU shipped in Safari 26 on macOS, iOS, iPadOS and visionOS in September 2025 — [web.dev: WebGPU is now supported in major browsers](https://web.dev/blog/webgpu-supported-major-browsers); [Cinevva news, 15 September 2025](https://app.cinevva.com/news/2025-09-15-safari-webgpu); WebKit bug reports show it is preview-only and switched off on macOS 15, working from macOS 26 — [WebKit bug 299237](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=299237)
- WebGPU is a W3C Candidate Recommendation Draft as of September 2026 — [Wikipedia: WebGPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WebGPU)
- Core WebGPU has `drawIndirect()` and `drawIndexedIndirect()`, each issuing a single draw from a buffer; multi-draw indirect exists only as the non-standard `chromium-experimental-multi-draw-indirect` feature behind Chrome developer flags since Chrome 131 — [Chrome for Developers: What's new in WebGPU 131](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/new-in-webgpu-131)
- A three.js r186 WebGPU and TSL project implements CDLOD: a CPU quadtree with frustum culling selects about 100 to 450 patches, draws them as one instanced grid and morphs vertices towards coarser levels to avoid cracks and popping; a compute pass culls trees on the GPU — [vvanghelue/webgpu-landscape-rendering](https://github.com/vvanghelue/webgpu-landscape-rendering)
- A three.js forum author reports a full-size planet generator on WebGPU using CDLOD, with compute shaders available since r156 and described as something WebGL 2 could not do — [three.js forum showcase](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/a-little-advertising-for-the-three-js-node-system-and-webgpu/67804); [earlier thread](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/good-progress-in-developing-a-real-ocean/55834)
- A Babylon.js developer with a real-scale planet using CDLOD on a quad-sphere reports replacing the CPU quadtree with a GPU-driven concurrent-binary-tree implementation on WebGPU — [Babylon.js forum: real scale planet](https://forum.babylonjs.com/t/wip-real-scale-planet/56910)
- Hable's adaptive compute tessellation ships with a live WebGPU viewer — [Filmic Worlds](https://filmicworlds.com/blog/adaptive-compute-tessellation/)

### Inferences
- A rock modelled as a cube-sphere displaced by 3D noise is a planet renderer's configuration in miniature. What planet renderers add, a quadtree per face so that resolution varies inside one object, pays off only when one object spans a large range of depths (a terrain, a planet, a cliff the camera walks along). For solids that cover a modest part of the screen the whole object can sit at one level, and the part worth borrowing is the morph: vertices of level L slide to where level L−1 would put them over the last part of level L's range, so the swap is invisible. That needs a vertex shader and 2:1 nested grids, nothing more.
- To morph between levels that are evaluated independently with different noise content, each vertex of level L needs two positions: its own, and the position it has in level L−1's surface (for an even vertex the coarse vertex itself, for an odd vertex the midpoint of its coarse neighbours, both using level L−1's band-limited recipe). This is the clipmap formulation (fine height and coarse height per vertex, blended by alpha) and costs one extra position attribute per vertex, or nothing if displacement is evaluated on the GPU.
- Two GPU evaluation patterns appear in the sources: noise evaluated in the vertex shader over a flat grid (the Godot project), and height or position tiles baked by a compute pass and then sampled (spherecraft, the OpenGL planet, Proland's GPU producers). For solids that are fixed once generated, the second pattern maps onto "one compute dispatch per asset per level writing a position and normal buffer", which would move the start-up evaluation from web workers to the GPU; the first costs the full noise stack per vertex per frame, which is the wrong trade on phones.
- On Safari only single indirect draws exist in core WebGPU, so GPU-driven patch selection has to compact an instance buffer and issue one instanced indirect draw of a shared grid, which is how the three.js CDLOD project already draws its patches.
- Concurrent binary trees are compute-only by design and thus compatible with WebGPU in principle (one developer reports doing it), but the machinery is justified for terrain and planets, not for many small closed solids. I found no reference WebGPU implementation to build on.
- Skirts are the superseded crack fix. Morphing (clipmaps, CDLOD) and conforming bisection (concurrent binary trees) are the two approaches that are both continuous and crack-free.

### Gaps
- Ulrich's original paper and Strugar's paper were not opened; morph formulas, error metrics and the exact text on skirts come from secondary descriptions.
- One developer write-up in the CDLOD results reported cracks when too many levels are packed into short ranges and found three to six levels worked well; I could not pin that to a specific page.
- The 2020 concurrent-binary-tree paper's known limitation (memory tied to maximum depth) is implied by the 2024 abstract but I did not retrieve its numbers; the 2024 paper's exact timings and required GPU features (atomics, bit operations) are unverified beyond secondary summaries.
- No source found measures any of these methods on Apple-silicon Macs or iPhones under Safari's WebGPU.
- Floating-point precision on small or very large spheres, and mismatch between noise evaluated on the GPU and on the CPU (for collision), were not covered by any source found.
- Unverified background: spherical clipmaps (Clasen and Hege 2006), the acko.net "Making Worlds" series on cube-sphere planets, and Frontier's talks on compute-shader planet generation for Elite Dangerous are relevant but the queries for them were cut off by the search budget.

## 3. Band-limiting procedural detail by level (dropping or fading noise octaves below the sampling rate; wavelet noise)

### Takeaway
The established way to make each level the prefiltered shape rather than an aliased one is frequency clamping: sum only the octaves the sampling rate can carry and fade the last one in smoothly rather than switching it. Cook and DeRose (2005) showed why a hard cut is not enough with Perlin-style noise (its bands leak well outside their nominal octave, so one must either alias or lose detail) and built wavelet noise, whose bands are almost perfectly band-limited, to remove that trade-off.

### Cited Findings
- Problem statement in "Wavelet Noise" (Cook and DeRose, SIGGRAPH 2005): Perlin builds patterns from bands of noise, each meant to cover a limited frequency range, but "each band actually contains a much wider range of frequencies than intended"; moreover a 2D slice through a band-limited 3D function "will in general not be band-limited", so the detail-versus-aliasing trade-off cannot be fixed just by band-limiting the 3D function — [Pixar: Wavelet Noise paper](https://graphics.pixar.com/library/WaveletNoise/paper.pdf)
- Wavelet noise is "almost perfectly band-limited, providing good detail with minimal aliasing", and 3D wavelet noise can texture a 2D surface while keeping its band-limited character — [Pixar: Wavelet Noise paper](https://graphics.pixar.com/library/WaveletNoise/paper.pdf)
- Construction: start from an image of random noise, downsample it to half size, upsample it back and subtract the result from the original; what remains is exactly the part that cannot be represented at half resolution, that is, one band; the filters come from wavelet analysis and correspond to the uniform quadratic B-spline basis; summing such bands partitions frequency space — [Wikipedia: Wavelet noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelet_noise); [Pixar: Wavelet Noise paper](https://graphics.pixar.com/library/WaveletNoise/paper.pdf)
- The related Pixar patent states the same defect of classic noise: each octave "often includes frequency components outside the desired range of frequencies of the octave" — [US patent 7,672,476, "Bandlimited noise for computer graphics"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7672476)
- Later literature summarises the result as "Cook and DeRose [2005] showed that Perlin noise is only approximately band limited, and is therefore subject to aliasing and detail loss" — [2011 Gabor noise paper, KU Leuven (identified from its file name as Lagae and Drettakis)](https://graphics.cs.kuleuven.be/publications/LD11FSGN/LD11FSGN_paper.pdf)
- Limits of clamping: band-limited isotropic noise can be isotropically filtered by frequency clamping, but because wavelet noise is isotropic, clamping alone cannot handle anisotropic filter footprints; Perlin noise is only weakly band-pass, which makes clamping hard to tune — [Goldberg, Zwicker and Durand, "Anisotropic Noise" (SIGGRAPH 2008)](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~zwicker/publications/AnisotropicNoise-SIG08.pdf); [Lagae et al., "A Survey of Procedural Noise Functions" (2010)](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~zwicker/publications/SurveyProceduralNoise-CGF10.pdf)
- Frequency clamping in practice: PBRT's `FBm()` takes the screen-space derivatives of the lookup point, computes an octave count from the larger derivative length and clamps it between zero and the maximum; its turbulence function gets the same treatment but, because of its derivative discontinuities, cannot be fully antialiased — [Physically Based Rendering, 3rd ed.: Noise](https://www.pbr-book.org/3ed-2018/Texture/Noise)
- Pixar's RenderMan notes call frequency clamping the next best thing to an analytic low-pass filter: octaves beyond the Nyquist limit are dropped or faded, and when the filter width is very large the whole function fades to its flat average with no detail — [RenderMan: Basic Antialiasing in RSL](https://renderman.pixar.com/resources/RenderMan_20/basicAntialiasing.html)
- Inigo Quilez: "While 'band-limiting' doesn't substitute proper filtering, it is a valid alternative approach to the problem of aliasing"; his band-limited fBm replaces the noise function in the octave loop with a self-limiting one, approximating filtered noise by blending each octave towards zero according to the ratio of its wavelength to the filter width — [iquilezles.org: bandlimiting](https://iquilezles.org/articles/bandlimiting/)
- Fractional octaves: a noise library pull request adds non-integer octave counts so the top octave fades smoothly as its frequency approaches Nyquist, avoiding the pop when the octave count changes — [rust-simd-noise pull request 76](https://github.com/verpeteren/rust-simd-noise/pull/76)
- A browser terrain project applies the same rule by distance: only octaves wider than about two pixels at distance t are kept, the last one is faded, and detail is reduced further at grazing angles because a pixel covers more ground there — [fzaninotto/mountains](https://github.com/fzaninotto/mountains)
- The same idea applied to geometry rather than shading: geometry clipmaps add synthesised fractal noise only at the level whose grid can carry it, on top of a smooth prediction from the coarser level — [GPU Gems 2, chapter 2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry); analytic displacement mapping selects the displacement mip level from the tessellation density to avoid under-sampling — [Nießner and Loop](https://niessnerlab.org/papers/2013/3analytic/niessner2013analytic.pdf)
- Volumetric procedural terrain uses the same ingredients: several octaves of noise sampled from 3D textures, with blocks of varying size and level of detail depending on the camera — [GPU Gems 3, chapter 1](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-i-geometry/chapter-1-generating-complex-procedural-terrains-using-gpu); [Geiss's project notes](https://geisswerks.com/about_terrain.html)

### Inferences
- For geometry, the sampling rate is the grid spacing h of a level, not the pixel: an octave of wavelength λ can be represented only if λ is at least 2h. A band-limited recipe for a level is therefore the sum of octaves each weighted by a smooth function of λ/h that reaches zero by the Nyquist limit. If the weight is a continuous function of a continuous level parameter (fractional octaves), the prefiltered shape itself changes continuously, and together with a vertex morph nothing is left to pop.
- "Dropping noise frequencies a level cannot carry" is frequency clamping. The literature adds three caveats: classic gradient noise leaks outside its octave, so a hard cut exactly at Nyquist both aliases and loses detail, and a fade over roughly an octave is safer; ridged and turbulence terms (absolute values) create creases that cannot be band-limited by removing octaves, so they look different at coarse levels whatever is done; and domain warping changes local frequency, so clamping by an octave's nominal frequency is only approximate.
- Detail removed from the geometry has to reappear in shading or the object visibly changes character: clipmaps blend normal maps across the same transition as the geometry, and analytic displacement picks the mip by tessellation density. Because the recipe is deterministic, a coarse level can carry normals computed from the full recipe rather than from its own coarse triangles.
- Wavelet noise itself is designed for texturing; I found no source applying it to displaced geometry levels, so its benefit there is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- The wavelet noise paper was not opened; its guidance on how bands should be weighted near the Nyquist limit is unverified.
- The classic attribution of frequency clamping (Norton, Rockwood and Skolmoski 1982; Ebert et al., "Texturing and Modeling: A Procedural Approach") was not confirmed by search.
- Unverified background: LEAN and LEADR mapping (Olano and Baker 2010; Dupuy et al. 2013) formalise moving sub-sample displacement into normal distribution and roughness; the query was cut off by the search budget.
- No source found quantifies visible popping from switching octave counts on displaced meshes, or compares hard dropping with fading on geometry.

## 4. Procedural generators with camera-adaptive resolution built in: Infinigen, Blender Cycles adaptive subdivision, Houdini

### Takeaway
All three offline systems use the same control, a target on-screen primitive size in pixels measured against a camera (Infinigen aims for mesh faces about one pixel across; Cycles has a dicing rate in pixels; Mantra and Karma have a dicing quality), and all three show the same two weaknesses: the mesh must be regenerated when the camera moves, which pops or flickers unless a fixed dicing camera or a spacetime mesh is used, and pixel-based dicing does not combine with instancing. Blender 5.0 (November 2025) took adaptive subdivision out of experimental status and added an object-space mode precisely for instanced meshes.

### Cited Findings

#### Infinigen (Princeton, CVPR 2023 onward)
- The paper describes dynamic resolution scaling: assets are evaluated at a level of detail such that mesh faces are approximately one pixel in size when rendered — [Raistrick et al., "Infinite Photorealistic Worlds using Procedural Generation" (arXiv 2306.09310)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09310)
- A follow-up from the same group describes the effect: Infinigen centres its grid spacing on the camera, with small cells near the camera and large ones far away, so that faces project to similar pixel sizes, and it re-extracts a view-dependent mesh when the camera moves — [Ma et al., arXiv 2312.08364](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.08364)
- A secondary summary of the paper puts the result at about 16 million polygons per 1080p frame, distributed by camera distance and viewing angle (not checked against the paper) — [alphaXiv summary](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2306.09310)
- The project states that "Infinigen does not use bump/normal-maps, full-transparency, or other techniques which fake geometric detail" and that all fine geometric detail is real, to guarantee accurate 3D ground truth — [infinigen.org](https://infinigen.org/)
- Terrain uses "Spherical Marching Cubes", an adaptation of marching cubes to spherical coordinates around the camera: a low-resolution pass divides the visible frustum into spherical blocks, a high-resolution pass runs dense signed-distance queries on the visible blocks and extracts the mesh, and the steps can be iterated to prevent holes; classic marching cubes at fixed intervals leaves geometry too sparse up close and too dense far away — [Infinigen paper, appendix F.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.09310)
- Limitation: the spherical mesher is view-dependent, so when the camera moves the mesh must be re-extracted; "If the camera changes its location significantly, visual artifacts will occur, producing a low-poly look" — [princeton-vl/OcMesher](https://github.com/princeton-vl/OcMesher)
- Successor 1, OcMesher: an octree built from a user-defined range of camera views yields a single mesh that renders without artefacts across those views; it is installed by default as of Infinigen v1.2.0 — [OcMesher repository](https://github.com/princeton-vl/OcMesher); [Ma et al., "View-Dependent Octree-based Mesh Extraction in Unbounded Scenes" (arXiv 2312.08364)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.08364)
- Successor 2, BinocMesher (Ma, Finkelstein and Deng, SIGGRAPH Asia 2025): independent meshes per view "may lead to popping artifacts during transitions"; the method builds a binary octree over space and time, extracts a 4D mesh by dual contouring and slices it per timestamp, which requires the camera path to be known in advance; the authors note that Infinigen otherwise uses extremely high-resolution meshes to supersample the scene, which is expensive — [arXiv 2509.13306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13306)

#### Blender Cycles adaptive subdivision
- Render settings: the Dicing Rate is the "size of micropolygons in pixels" for the final render and for the viewport; Offscreen Scale multiplies the dicing rate for geometry outside the camera view (lower values give better reflections and shadows of off-screen objects, higher values use less memory); a Max Subdivisions setting caps the level regardless of the rate — [Blender 4.5 LTS manual: Subdivision](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.5/render/cycles/render_settings/subdivision.html)
- Per object, a Dicing Scale multiplies the scene dicing rate; a listed limitation is that instances are not tessellated individually: the original object is tessellated once and duplicated — [Blender 4.3 manual: Adaptive Subdivision](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.3/render/cycles/object_settings/adaptive_subdiv.html)
- Blender 5.0 release notes: adaptive subdivision is available by default in the Subdivision Surface modifier and is no longer an experimental feature; a new object-space option sets edge length in object units instead of screen pixels — [Blender 5.0 release notes: Cycles](https://developer.blender.org/docs/release_notes/5.0/cycles/); [CG Channel coverage, 19 November 2025](https://www.cgchannel.com/?p=171940)
- Current manual: the modifier's adaptive subdivision panel has two modes, pixel space (a target polygon size on screen) and object space (a target edge length); object space is required for instanced meshes because instancing does not work well with pixel-space subdivision — [Blender 5.3 manual: Subdivision Surface modifier](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/modeling/modifiers/generate/subdivision_surface.html)

#### Houdini (Mantra and Karma)
- Mantra's default mode is micropolygon scanline rendering; when displacements are shared between instances, the dicing quality used is that of the instance closest to the camera — [SideFX: Mantra rendering properties](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/props/mantra.html)
- Karma has a Dicing Camera, "a camera that is used for dicing complicated surfaces. This can provide consistent dicing of surfaces when the viewing camera is moving" — [SideFX: Karma LOP](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/lop/karma.html)
- Karma dices instances from the instance nearest the camera, and "animated camera moves can thus sometimes cause flickering in the dicing"; dicing quality is set in render geometry settings with a documented default of 0.5 — [SideFX: Karma User Guide, Geometry](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/solaris/kug/geometry.html)
- A user report on Karma XPU in Houdini 20 found a dicing value of 1 too heavy and 0.35 produced artefacts on surface far from the camera (anecdotal; it also gives the default as 1, which conflicts with the user guide) — [Medium post on Karma XPU oceans](https://berkerdag.medium.com/karma-xpu-and-guided-oceans-in-houdini-20-3711d4203f38)

### Inferences
- The one engine-wide rule already exists in production offline tools and it is a single number: primitive size in pixels. What differs for a game is that the camera path is unknown and the result must be reused across frames and instances.
- These systems regenerate geometry per view and accept (or work around) temporal inconsistency: a fixed dicing camera in Cycles and Karma, an octree over a known set of views in OcMesher, a spacetime mesh for a known path in BinocMesher. None of those is available to a game, which points to nested levels with a continuous transition between them rather than re-meshing per view.
- Pixel-space dicing is incompatible with instancing in all three (Blender tessellates the original once and now requires object-space mode; Mantra and Karma use the nearest instance). A game with many instances of the same rock must likewise define levels in object units and choose among them per instance.
- Infinigen's position, all detail as real geometry with one-pixel faces, is the limiting case and is affordable only offline; it marks the point at which a real-time engine hands sub-pixel detail to shading.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Infinigen repository or documentation, so the per-asset mechanism (parameter names, how plants, rocks and creatures receive their target face size, defaults) is unverified. Unverified background: the code has a placement "detail" module that computes a target face size from camera distance and adapts subdivision or remeshes to it.
- Whether Infinigen's exporters bake a fixed resolution or produce levels of detail for game engines was not established.
- Default values of Cycles' dicing rates were not in the snippets (unverified background: 1 pixel for render, 8 pixels for viewport).
- Houdini's "Remesh to Camera" geometry node appeared in results by title only ([SideFX documentation](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/remeshtocamera.html)); its behaviour was not read. Houdini height fields and PolyReduce-based level generation were not researched.

## 5. Trees and plants: LOD built into the generator (Weber and Penn, Deussen, SpeedTree, Remolar, Neubert); LOD that follows botanical structure; what generalises

### Takeaway
Tree LOD has always lived in the generator rather than in a mesh simplifier, because a tree is aggregate detail that edge-collapse simplification thins out or destroys. The recurring toolkit is small: remove elements in a fixed priority order (random for leaves, structural for branches), enlarge the survivors to conserve covered area, make each removal continuous (shrink a branch to its centre line, merge two leaves into one), and at the limit change primitive (polygons to lines and points in 1995 and 2002, to billboards in SpeedTree, to voxels in Nanite Foliage in 2025). SpeedTree's vertex-interpolated "smooth LOD" has largely given way to engine cross-fades in its current format.

### Cited Findings

#### Weber and Penn 1995
- "Creation and Rendering of Realistic Trees" (SIGGRAPH 95) has a section "Degradation at Range" giving a method for seamlessly degrading the tree geometry at long ranges so that large numbers of trees can be drawn: the tree "will re-interpret stem meshes as lines and leaf polygons as points" — [paper copy, Duke University](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/fall02/cps124/resources/p119-weber.pdf)
- Each tree is split into four geometric descriptions, three stem levels and the leaves; "stems beyond the third level are grouped with the third level", since finer stem levels are rarely visible at long range and are often hidden by leaves; the paper tabulates how many level-1 and level-2 lines and leaf points are drawn in each range band, with counts falling as range grows — [paper copy](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/fall02/cps124/resources/p119-weber.pdf)
- Later literature characterises the scheme as replacing textured primitives gradually with points and lines as the camera recedes, with an automatic size criterion for which parts disappear, and criticises that at longer ranges individual branches and leaves "simply disappear", which can cause visible artefacts (attribution among the following two documents not verified) — [point-based rendering of trees](https://www.academia.edu/68851863/Point_based_rendering_of_trees); [review, HAL](https://hal-lirmm.ccsd.cnrs.fr/lirmm-01925641/document)
- The range-degradation part is rarely reimplemented: one C# port explicitly leaves it out and another partial implementation calls sections of the paper confusing — [aidannewsome/weber-penn-net](https://github.com/aidannewsome/weber-penn-net); [levork/arbre](https://github.com/levork/arbre)

#### Deussen et al. 2002
- "Interactive Visualization of Complex Plant Ecosystems" (Deussen, Colditz, Stamminger, Drettakis; IEEE Visualization 2002, pp. 219 to 226) represents plant models and populations by compact point and line sets, which "enables us to show landscapes faithfully using only a limited percentage of primitives" — [paper, University of Konstanz](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/e16ccec7-74c4-4ebc-8729-58e777ce67cd/content); [publication page](https://graphics.uni-konstanz.de/publikationen/Deussen2002Interactivevisualizationcomplex/index.html)
- Its level of detail blends so that more and more polygons of a plant are represented by points and lines, with the data reduction adapted to the visual importance of the geometric objects — [paper](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/e16ccec7-74c4-4ebc-8729-58e777ce67cd/content)

#### SpeedTree's smooth LOD
- Branches and fronds "can use traditional popping between LODs, but they also employ a scaling technique that gradually shrinks small outlying geometry toward its center line"; the geometry is scaled away when it is only a few pixels in size, so the change is hard to notice — [SpeedTree runtime SDK manual (Unity-hosted): Level of detail](https://docs.unity3d.com/speedtree-runtime-sdk/manual/level-of-detail.html)
- Leaf LOD "is based on using fewer and bigger leaf instances as the camera moves away from the tree" — [SpeedTree 9 SDK documentation](https://docs9.speedtree.com/sdk/doku.php?id=level-of-detail); kept leaves "are scaled up to fill in the empty space left by those that are removed", with the scale point computed automatically; a Keep property sets the minimum leaf count at the lowest level — [SpeedTree Modeler documentation: LOD](https://docs.speedtree.com/doku.php?id=lod)
- The Modeler analyses the tree to find hidden interior geometry and removes that first; branch reduction is driven by a curve per geometry type, and an Optimization property lets the tool decide where to remove segments while keeping the overall shape; dynamic LOD properties such as Weight and Grow Scale tune the result — [SpeedTree 8 Modeler documentation: LOD](https://docs8.speedtree.com/modeler/doku.php?id=lod); [SpeedTree 9 Modeler: tree generator](https://docs9.speedtree.com/modeler/doku.php?id=tree_generator)
- Limits: "No smooth transition algorithm is available when switching between meshes", so artists must model geometrically similar meshes; segment reduction and mesh swaps can pop; the final step from 3D model to billboard is a cross-fade — [SpeedTree Modeler manual (Unity-hosted): Level of detail](https://docs.unity3d.com/speedtree-modeler/manual/level-of-detail.html)
- Automatic LOD exists only in the real-time products; the VFX editions have no LOD and use a Resolution setting instead — [SpeedTree Modeler documentation: LOD](https://docs.speedtree.com/doku.php?id=lod)
- Current state: for the ST9 format in Unreal, "All smooth LOD transitions are done using UE's crossfaded LOD"; the legacy importer offered a choice between "SpeedTree-style LOD (with growing/shrinking geometry)" and Unreal's cross-faded LOD — [Importing into Unreal Engine (ST9)](https://docs.unity3d.com/speedtree-modeler/manual/import-to-unreal.html); [legacy import documentation](https://docs9.speedtree.com/modeler/doku.php?id=impue4)
- In Unity, classic SpeedTree models store, for each vertex, "its position in the current and the next LOD" for interpolation, but for .st9 files Unity changes LODs without a smooth transition — [Unity manual: LOD Group transitions](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.3/Documentation/Manual/lod/lod-transitions-lod-group.html)

#### Foliage simplification by leaf collapse (Remolar et al. and followers)
- The foliage simplification algorithm replaces two leaves with a single larger leaf of similar position and shape whose area is close to the combined area of the two originals — ["Fast Rendering of Leaves" (Remolar, Chover, Ribelles and Belmonte among the authors per search; the file name suggests Rebollo as first author, 2007)](http://www.gametools.org/archives/publications/UJI_Rebollo07_FRL.pdf)
- That work keeps the high-resolution vertices on the GPU and renders the subset needed for each approximation — [same paper](http://www.gametools.org/archives/publications/UJI_Rebollo07_FRL.pdf)
- Followers build view-dependent hierarchies of leaf collapses for sparse organs such as leaves, flowers and fruit — ["View-Dependent Hierarchical Foliage Simplification"](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-540-73011-8_7); ["Viewpoint-Driven Simplification of Plant and Tree Foliage"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7512728/)

#### Stochastic pruning (Cook et al. 2007) and Neubert et al. 2011
- "Stochastic Simplification of Aggregate Detail" (Cook, Halstead, Planck, Ryu; ACM TOG 26(3), 2007) observes that existing simplification techniques work best with element detail and not with aggregate detail produced by a large number of elements; it randomly selects a subset of the elements and alters them statistically to preserve the overall appearance; the amount of simplification can depend on screen size, motion blur and depth of field — [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1276377.1276476)
- A real-time restatement: build the mesh of N elements (leaves as quads), sort the elements once in random order (Fisher-Yates), compute from distance the proportion U to render, and draw the first N×U elements with their area scaled by 1/U; the technique was developed for the desert scenes in Cars — [Miles Macklin: "Stochastic pruning for real-time LOD"](https://blog.mmacklin.com/2010/01/12/stochastic-pruning-for-real-time-lod/)
- Neubert, Pirk, Deussen and Dachsbacher, "Improved Model- and View-Dependent Pruning of Large Botanical Scenes" (Computer Graphics Forum 30(6), 2011, pp. 1708 to 1718) applies model-specific geometry reduction functions and optimised scaling functions, and extends the originally purely stochastic prioritisation to view-optimised geometry selection that takes occlusion into account — [University of Konstanz publication page](https://graphics.uni-konstanz.de/publikationen/Neubert2011ImprovedModelView/index.html)

#### LOD that follows botanical structure
- "Procedural multiresolution for plant and tree rendering" argues that geometry simplification fails to preserve the visual structure of a tree and instead builds procedural models from parametric L-systems that reflect the tree's visual structure at different resolution levels — [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/602330.602336)
- Related work builds multiresolution models of branches as well as sparse organs — [Zhang and Deng, "Level of Detail Technique for Plant Models"](https://nlpr.ia.ac.cn/2006papers/gnkw/nk33.pdf); and view-dependent pruning on the GPU — ["View-dependent pruning for real-time rendering of trees"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0097849310001834)

#### Current engine practice: Nanite Foliage (Unreal Engine 5.7, November 2025, experimental)
- Epic's foliage pipeline combines Nanite Assemblies (trees built from instanced parts), Nanite Skinning (wind through bones rather than world position offset) and Nanite Voxels, which in Epic's description relayed by press coverage "automatically and efficiently draw millions of tiny, overlapping elements that read as a solid mass at distance, without cross fades, pops, or the need to author LODs"; Epic labels the system experimental — [Digital Production on Unreal Engine 5.7](https://digitalproduction.com/2025/11/12/unreal-engine-5-7-foliage-pcg-and-in-editor-ai/); [Tom Looman: 5.7 performance highlights](https://tomlooman.com/unreal-engine-5-7-performance-highlights/)
- Epic's documentation says Nanite Voxels "can preserve the general silhouette of aggregate geometry (disconnected pieces) during simplification at distance", with voxel-enabled meshes grouped into clusters of at most 128 bricks of 4×4×4 voxels — [Epic documentation: Nanite Foliage](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/nanite-foliage?lang=en-US)
- Why it was needed: when Nanite simplifies aggregate geometry in the distance "it eventually needs to start removing some of these disjoint elements completely", which thins canopies; the earlier remedy was a Preserve Area option — [Epic documentation: Nanite Virtualized Geometry](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine)

### Inferences
- The methods reduce to four operators on a hierarchy of parts. Drop parts in a fixed priority order: random for leaves (Cook), structural for branches (Weber and Penn's grouping of fine stem levels, SpeedTree's interior-first removal). Compensate: scale survivors by 1/U or merge two leaves into one of equal area, so covered area and overall brightness stay constant. Make each removal continuous: shrink a branch to its centre line before it goes, or move vertices to their next-level positions. Change primitive at the limit: lines and points, billboards, voxels.
- Dropping the highest branching order first is what Weber and Penn's four descriptions amount to (everything beyond the third level is lumped with the third and is the first to be reduced), and it is what a per-species function with hand-written counts per level encodes by hand. A single rule can replace those counts: order the elements once (branch order, then size, then a random key for leaves), draw the first N×U of them, with U a single engine-wide function of projected size, and scale the survivors. Only the ordering is species-specific and the generator already knows it.
- The mechanism needs an ordered vertex or instance buffer, a draw range per instance and a scale in the vertex shader. Nothing in it requires tessellation, mesh shaders or multi-draw, so it fits WebGPU as it stands.
- Tubes have sampling rates like a terrain grid (radial and lengthwise segments) and can be diced from projected radius; below about a pixel of width a tube is a line or ribbon, which is exactly Weber and Penn's reinterpretation of stems as lines.
- The fact that Epic first added "Preserve Area" and then a voxel representation for foliage, and that SpeedTree never offered smooth transitions between imported meshes, is strong evidence that generic mesh simplification is the wrong tool for trees and that the generator (or a volumetric far field) has to do the work.
- The pattern generalises to any procedural hierarchy of parts: each node needs a projected size below which it is absorbed into its parent, a continuous way to disappear, and a rule by which the parent takes over its area and colour.

## 6. Implicit, SDF and voxel-based procedural content and its inherent LOD (Dreams, sparse voxel octrees and DAGs, Transvoxel, dual contouring); advantages and drawbacks for a browser engine

### Takeaway
Implicit and voxel representations get level of detail almost for free, because resampling the field on a coarser grid is the prefiltered shape and topology never has to be preserved, and they handle any shape, including aggregates. The price moves elsewhere: evaluating the field, extracting or splatting a surface per level, storing bricks, and closing seams between chunks of different resolution (Transvoxel's transition cells, dual contouring's seam meshes). The sources found give only anecdotal evidence about browsers, and it points to per-pixel ray marching being costly on weak GPUs; the strongest current use of voxels in a triangle engine is as the far-field stage only (Nanite Foliage).

### Cited Findings

#### Dreams (Media Molecule)
- Alex Evans presented "Learning from Failure: a Survey of Promising, Unconventional and Mostly Abandoned Renderers for 'Dreams PS4'" in the SIGGRAPH 2015 Advances in Real-Time Rendering course, covering the failed attempts on the way to the final engine — [Media Molecule blog](https://www.mediamolecule.com/blog/article/siggraph_2015); [SIGGRAPH 2015 Advances course page](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/)
- From the abstract: the engine "runs almost entirely on the PS4's compute unit (no triangles!); it builds on scenes described through Operationally Transformed CSG trees, which are evaluated on-the-fly to high resolution signed distance fields, from which we generate dense multi-resolution point clouds" — [SIGGRAPH 2015 Advances course page](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/)
- One abandoned renderer adapted the brick tree of GigaVoxels, choosing a cut through the tree by view distance; "memory for all the gigavoxel bricks was also an issue", which led back to point clouds; a scene became a cloud of sculpts, each of which is a point cloud, each point of which becomes a small point cloud itself when it gets close to the camera ("a cloud of clouds of point clouds") — [DualShockers report on the talk](https://www.dualshockers.com/media-molecules-ps4-exclusive-dreams-gets-tons-of-wip-screenshots-showing-failure-and-success/)
- Forum readers of the talk report that the fields are stored as 8×8×8 bricks in a volume texture, and that there is a separate point cloud, clustering and bounding hierarchy for each mip level of the filtered field; they also report that the splats are drawn by a compute shader rather than by tracing primary rays (community summary) — [Beyond3D forum thread](https://forum.beyond3d.com/threads/signed-distance-field-rendering-pros-and-cons-as-used-in-ps4-title-dreams-spawn.57006/page-2)
- Conflicting accounts of the shipped renderer: a later community summary says it used splats for "fluff" and ray-marched solid distance-field bricks for "hulls" (unverified) — [student dissertation citing it](https://jambuttenshaw.github.io/devportfolio/Dissertation.pdf)

#### Sparse voxel octrees and DAGs
- "Efficient Sparse Voxel Octrees" (Laine and Karras, I3D 2010): "We augment the voxel data with novel contour information that increases geometric resolution, allows more compact encoding of smooth surfaces, and accelerates ray casts" — [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1730804.1730814)
- "High Resolution Sparse Voxel DAGs" (Kämpe, Sintorn, Assarsson, SIGGRAPH 2013) generalises the octree to a directed acyclic graph so identical subtrees share nodes; "the number of nodes is reduced by one to three orders of magnitude" even in highly irregular scenes; for the Epic Citadel scene, 19 billion voxels need 945 MB of GPU memory where a sparse voxel octree would need 5.1 GB without counting pointers — [paper, Chalmers](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/~uffe/HighResolutionSparseVoxelDAGs.pdf)
- Voxels were considered and prototyped for Nanite: Karis's 2022 keynote describes voxels as having the advantage of not showing cracks between pieces — [Karis, "Journey to Nanite", HPG 2022](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf); a developer's post describes a Nanite voxel prototype built on 4×4×4 bricks — [Victor Careil on X](https://x.com/phyronnaz/status/1657016107037863936); and a voxel far field shipped for foliage in Unreal Engine 5.7 (see section 5) — [Epic documentation: Nanite Foliage](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/nanite-foliage?lang=en-US)

#### Transvoxel (Lengyel)
- The Transvoxel algorithm dates from 2009 and is described in Eric Lengyel's 2010 dissertation at UC Davis, "Voxel-Based Terrain for Real-Time Virtual Simulations", and in "Transition Cells for Dynamic Multiresolution Marching Cubes" (Journal of Graphics, GPU, and Game Tools 15(2); sources give 2010 or 2011) — [transvoxel.org](https://transvoxel.org/); [EricLengyel/Transvoxel](https://github.com/EricLengyel/Transvoxel)
- It "inserts special transition cells in between regular cells along the boundary between voxel data sampled at one resolution and voxel data sampled at exactly half that resolution" — [transvoxel.org](https://transvoxel.org/)
- A transition cell uses the nine samples of the high-resolution face (3×3) against the four of the low-resolution face (2×2), giving 512 distinct cases that reduce to 73 equivalence classes (merged to 56 in the data tables); regular cells have the usual 256 cases — [Transvoxel reference sheet](https://transvoxel.org/Transvoxel.pdf); [EricLengyel/Transvoxel](https://github.com/EricLengyel/Transvoxel)
- Without transition cells, blocks at different resolutions "generally do not match at the blocks' junctions, inducing visible holes" — [transvoxel crate documentation](https://docs.rs/crate/transvoxel/2.0.0/source/README.md)
- A 2026 browser open-world blog calls Transvoxel the standard solution for volumetric terrain "because geomorphing only works for heightmaps", and lists implementation pitfalls (vertex numbering differs from regular cells; winding must match or back-face culling removes the seam triangles) — [Cinevva engineering blog, February 2026](https://app.cinevva.com/blog/2026-02-25-open-world-browser-part-09-transvoxel-first-cut)

#### Dual contouring at several resolutions
- "Dual Contouring of Hermite Data" (Ju, Losasso, Schaefer, Warren, SIGGRAPH 2002) contours a signed octree with Hermite data on its edges; it "imposes no constraints on the octree (such as being a restricted octree) and requires no 'crack patching'" — [paper, Rice University](https://www.cs.rice.edu/~jwarren/papers/dualcontour.pdf)
- "Manifold Dual Contouring" further guarantees a manifold mesh even under adaptive simplification — [IEEE TVCG 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/TVCG.2007.1012)
- In a chunked world the guarantee stops at chunk borders: cracks "are caused by the contouring each chunk's octree without considering the neighbouring octrees"; the fix is to generate seam meshes from the border nodes of neighbouring chunks, selected according to each neighbour's current level — [Nick Gildea: "Dual Contouring: Seams & LOD for Chunked Terrain" (2014)](http://ngildea.blogspot.com/2014/09/dual-contouring-chunked-terrain.html)

#### Browser evidence (anecdotal)
- The author of a WebGPU distance-field ray marcher reports that "I can't have more than maybe 20 objects in the scene without noticeable slowdown" — [Batteson blog, 2023](https://blog.batteson.com/2023/12/17/raymarching-webgpu-stack-machine)
- A WebGPU voxel fractal renderer runs on integrated graphics but "the performance is generally very slow" — [AddisonPrairie/WebGPU-Voxel-Fractals](https://github.com/AddisonPrairie/WebGPU-Voxel-Fractals)

### Inferences
- The inherent LOD of implicit content is real but relocates the work: the field must be evaluated (in Dreams, edit lists evaluated by compute), a surface or point set must be produced per level, and chunked worlds need seam handling. Transvoxel and seam meshes solve cracks between levels, not popping; continuous transitions still need cross-fades or stochastic blending, because vertices of meshes extracted at different resolutions do not correspond.
- For closed solids defined by layered noise, switching from a displaced cube-sphere to a density field with marching cubes or dual contouring would buy overhangs and caves, at the cost of the nested regular grid that makes vertex morphing trivial. For shapes that are star-shaped about a centre, the displaced cube-sphere is the cheaper and smoother choice.
- For Safari on phones, the evidence found favours staying with triangles as the base representation: hardware rasterisation of meshes is the path mobile GPUs are built for, per-pixel ray marching of procedural fields is the most expensive option, and compute-rasterised point splats are unproven there. Voxels or points are most plausible as a far-field stage for aggregates, as Nanite Foliage uses them.
- Flat-faced, hard-edged content is poorly served by plain density grids; only Hermite-data methods (dual contouring) keep sharp edges, and coarse levels round them off regardless.
- Sparse voxel DAG compression relies on repeated structure and static content; it is a storage format for large static scenes rather than a way to derive levels from a recipe.

## 7. Geometry images and regular remeshing (Gu, Gortler, Hoppe 2002) and displaced subdivision surfaces as ways to turn any surface into regular grids with mip-like levels

### Takeaway
Geometry images remesh an arbitrary surface into a completely regular grid of positions so that level of detail becomes mip-mapping, and displaced subdivision surfaces express a shape as a smooth base plus a scalar displacement with a built-in pyramid of levels; both deliver exactly the nested regular levels a procedural cube-sphere already has by construction. Their limits are topology and seams: a single chart needs a cut and distorts, several charts need "zippering" that breaks when parts are drawn at different resolutions, and Nanite's author abandoned the idea for general content because detail was lost and hard-surface shapes did not fit.

### Cited Findings
- "Geometry Images" (Gu, Gortler, Hoppe; SIGGRAPH 2002, ACM TOG 21(3), pp. 355 to 361) remeshes an arbitrary surface onto a completely regular structure: the mesh is cut open, the resulting chart is parametrised onto a square, and the geometry is sampled on a regular 2D grid; normals and colours are stored as further square images sharing the same domain, so the result is a connectivity-free resampling — [Hoppe: geometry images project page](https://hhoppe.com/proj/gim/); [paper](https://hhoppe.com/gim.pdf); [Harvard DASH record](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/2641681)
- "Level-of-detail rendering is implemented by mip-mapping the geometry image" — [paper](https://hhoppe.com/gim.pdf)
- Limitations stated around the method: finding a good cut is one of the main challenges, the reconstruction must match exactly along the cut, a whole surface is mapped to one square, and models with disconnected components need a separate geometry image each — [paper copy](https://www.cs.hunter.cuny.edu/~ioannis/3DP_F03/PAPERS/gortler.pdf); [multi-chart geometry images patent text](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7586488)
- "Multi-Chart Geometry Images" (Sander et al., Eurographics Symposium on Geometry Processing 2003) uses an atlas of charts instead of a single square; because chart-based atlases reconstruct discontinuously at chart borders (visible cracks), it adds a zippering algorithm that unifies the discretised chart boundaries into a watertight mesh, with special handling of cut nodes where more than two charts meet — [paper](https://hhoppe.com/mcgim.pdf)
- "Spherical Parametrization and Remeshing" (Praun and Hoppe) parametrises a genus-zero surface onto a sphere and resamples it onto a regular 2D grid (a spherical geometry image) by unfolding the sphere over an octahedron domain — [paper](https://hhoppe.com/sphereparam.pdf); [Hoppe and Praun, "Shape Compression using Spherical Geometry Images"](https://hhoppe.com/shapecompr.pdf)
- Geometry videos decimate the geometry image to obtain a lower-resolution version, controlling level of detail through mesh resolution and frame rate — ["Geometry videos" (Sander, McMillan, Gortler, Hoppe and co-author)](https://hhoppe.com/gvid.pdf)
- "Displaced Subdivision Surfaces" (Lee, Moreton, Hoppe; SIGGRAPH 2000) represents a detailed surface as a scalar-valued displacement over a smooth domain surface, with both the domain surface and the displacement function defined in one subdivision framework; benefits listed are compression, editing, animation, scalability and adaptive rendering, with level of detail drawn from the displacement pyramid — [Hoppe: displaced subdivision surfaces project page](https://hhoppe.com/proj/dss/); [paper](http://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs468-01-winter/papers/lmh-dss-00.pdf)
- A January 2009 Graphic Rants post, "Virtual Geometry Images", proposed pairing every virtual-texture page with a geometry-image page, so that each use of a patch is a fixed-size grid of quads that works with instancing when positions are fetched from a texture; it notes that multi-chart geometry images have "a complicated zipping procedure that is also invalid if a part is rendered at a different resolution" — [Graphic Rants, January 2009](https://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2009/01)
- Karis's Nanite talks record why this line was dropped: geometry images are essentially vector displacement maps relative to the origin, geometry "isn't trivially filterable", and subdivision surfaces only add detail and cannot reduce it — [Karis et al., "Nanite: A Deep Dive" (SIGGRAPH 2021)](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- Secondary notes on his 2022 keynote say he abandoned geometry images and displacement in favour of irregular meshes because artists found detail lost in practice, the approach assumed simple topology, and hard-surface mechanical objects did not fit (notes in Chinese, not checked against the slides) — [notes on "Journey to Nanite"](https://dorgon.horizon-studio.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/nanite_journey_note_by_dorgon.pdf); [slides](https://www.highperformancegraphics.org/slides22/Journey_to_Nanite.pdf)
- The displaced-subdivision idea was productised as NVIDIA Micro-Mesh (base triangles plus power-of-two displacement grids) and is still an active research topic, for example a CVPR 2024 method that constructs micro-meshes while limiting the level difference between adjacent base faces to one to stay watertight — [Dou et al., "Differentiable Micro-Mesh Construction"](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Dou_Differentiable_Micro-Mesh_Construction_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- A cube-sphere whose six faces are regular grids of displaced positions is a six-chart geometry image whose chart borders match by construction, because both faces sample the same 3D function at the same points. Its power-of-two resolutions are the mip chain. Procedural rocks are therefore already in the regular form that this literature works hard to reach from arbitrary meshes; what remains to be added is only the transition between mips.
- There are two ways to build the chain and they trade against each other. Subsampling keeps coarse vertices exactly where the fine level had them, which nests perfectly (even vertices need no morph) but is point sampling and aliases. Re-evaluating a band-limited recipe per level gives the prefiltered shape, but then every vertex moves between levels and a morph is needed.
- Zippering being invalid across resolutions is the same lesson as the tessellation rule in section 1: seams are only safe when both sides sample the seam identically, which a single shared 3D function gives for free and a baked atlas does not.
- The approach does not extend to shapes of higher genus, thin parts or sharp features without cuts and charts, so it cannot be the engine-wide rule for buildings and furniture. It does extend to anything expressible as a smooth base plus scalar displacement, which includes trunks and limbs (a tube plus bark).

### Gaps
- How Gu et al. avoid cracks between geometry images drawn at different resolutions was cut off in the snippet; the paper was not opened.
- Praun and Hoppe's use of other polyhedral domains (by background knowledge the paper also covers a cube and a tetrahedron, which would map directly onto a cube-sphere) was not confirmed by search.
- "Smooth Geometry Images" (Losasso, Hoppe, Schaefer, Warren 2003; [PDF](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/schaefer/research/smooth_geoimages.pdf)) and a 2026 preprint on compact base meshes for displacement mapping ([arXiv 2606.22880](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22880)) appeared by title only.
- The 2009 post's author is given as Karis by context; the search snippet did not show the byline.

## 8. When is "generate each level from the recipe" worse than "simplify the finished mesh"? (per-generator effort against a generic tool, consistency between levels, shapes that are not parametric)

### Takeaway
Generating each level from the recipe is the better choice wherever the recipe has a sampling rate (grids, tubes, element counts) and is the only good choice for aggregates such as foliage, where simplification demonstrably fails. It is the worse choice where the shape is a set of discrete, sharp features with no resolution to turn down (buildings, furniture): there the options are hand-written or grammar-level LOD rules, which is the per-generator effort a generic simplifier avoids, and the one published route to automating it works by rewriting the generator's rules rather than by a universal law. Independently generated levels also do not correspond to each other, so they pop unless the generator is made to nest them or supply morph targets.

### Cited Findings
- Generic simplification's advantage is that it works on whatever mesh it is given and can reduce detail, which amplification-only schemes cannot: Nanite's authors rejected subdivision surfaces because subdivision only adds detail and cannot reduce it, and argue that no alternative representation to triangles offers higher quality or speed, while granting that other representations have legitimate uses — [Karis et al., "Nanite: A Deep Dive" (SIGGRAPH 2021)](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2021/Karis_Nanite_SIGGRAPH_Advances_2021_final.pdf)
- The two are complementary in the current state of the art: in Nanite one mesh sees simplification where patches are small and tessellation where they are large — [Graphic Rants: "Nanite + Reyes"](https://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2026/02/nanite-reyes.html)
- Where simplification fails: existing simplification "works best with element detail" and not with aggregate detail made of many elements — [Cook et al. 2007](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1276377.1276476); geometry simplification fails to preserve the visual structure of a tree — ["Procedural multiresolution for plant and tree rendering"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/602330.602336); Nanite's simplifier eventually removes disjoint elements entirely and thins canopies, which Epic patched with Preserve Area and then with voxels — [Epic documentation: Nanite Virtualized Geometry](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine); [Epic documentation: Nanite Foliage](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/nanite-foliage?lang=en-US)
- Generator-side LOD is the commercial norm for trees and is automatic there: SpeedTree's real-time products derive levels from the tree model itself, with smooth transitions only for geometry the generator made, not between imported meshes — [SpeedTree Modeler documentation: LOD](https://docs.speedtree.com/doku.php?id=lod); [SpeedTree Modeler manual: Level of detail](https://docs.unity3d.com/speedtree-modeler/manual/level-of-detail.html)
- Buildings, the manual route: in CGA shape grammars rules start from a crude volumetric mass model and add detail in later steps — [Müller et al., "Procedural Modeling of Buildings" (SIGGRAPH 2006)](https://peterwonka.net/Publications/pdfs/2006.SG.Mueller.ProceduralModelingOfBuildings.final.pdf); in CityEngine the level of detail is an attribute the rule author declares (`attr LOD = 1`) and branches on by hand, with a low-complexity and a high-complexity path — [Esri CityEngine tutorial 6: basic shape grammar](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/2021.0/tutorials/tutorial-6-basic-shape-grammar.htm)
- Esri's export notes recommend confining the LOD conditions to separate "asset loader" rules so the attribute does not spread through the whole rule set, and its Facade Wizard generates rules whose split shapes become windows, doors and cornices "depending on the desired level of detail" — [CityEngine model export application notes](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/2019.1/help/help-model-export-application-notes.htm); [CityEngine tutorial 13a: Facade Wizard](https://doc.arcgis.com/en/cityengine/latest/tutorials/tutorial-13a-facade-wizard-basic-facade-creation.htm)
- Buildings, the automated route: "Customizable LoD for Procedural Architecture" (Besuievsky and Patow, Computer Graphics Forum 2013) proposes a semantic, procedural LoD for any rule-based building definition; it extends the grammar with LoD-oriented commands and inserts them into the artist's rule set automatically by graph rewriting; simplification can be driven by distance, projected screen size, semantic selection or a scripted criterion — [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cgf.12141); [author copy](https://ggg.udg.edu/publicacions/UsersWebs/customizableLod/CustomizableLoD.pdf)
- Consistency over time and between levels is the standing weakness of regeneration: independent meshes per view "may lead to popping artifacts during transitions" — [BinocMesher, arXiv 2509.13306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13306); Infinigen's view-dependent mesh shows "a low-poly look" once the camera moves away from the view it was built for — [OcMesher repository](https://github.com/princeton-vl/OcMesher); camera moves can cause flickering in Karma's dicing — [SideFX: Karma User Guide, Geometry](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/solaris/kug/geometry.html); Nanite's tessellation pattern pops when integer factors change — [Hable, SIGGRAPH 2026 slides](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2026/content/Adaptive%20Tessellation%20and%20Subdivision_7.pdf)
- Methods that do achieve continuity rely on nesting: CDLOD transforms the finer mesh completely into the coarser one before swapping — [CDLOD paper copy](https://aggrobird.com/files/cdlod_latest.pdf); classic SpeedTree stores each vertex's position in the current and the next level — [Unity manual: LOD Group transitions](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.3/Documentation/Manual/lod/lod-transitions-lod-group.html)
- Per-instance choice forces object-space definitions: Blender had to add an object-space mode because pixel-space dicing does not work with instances — [Blender 5.3 manual: Subdivision Surface modifier](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/modeling/modifiers/generate/subdivision_surface.html)
- Generator-side dicing gives up control that a simplifier's error metric would provide: Nanite tessellation needs a user-supplied displacement bound and offers no programmable dicing rate — [Graphic Rants: "Nanite + Reyes"](https://graphicrants.blogspot.com/2026/02/nanite-reyes.html)

### Inferences

Comparison assembled from the cited findings in all sections:

| Criterion | Generate each level from the recipe | Simplify the finished mesh |
|---|---|---|
| Effort | Every generator must expose and honour a detail parameter | One tool, written once, works on any triangle output |
| Parametric surfaces (displaced cube-sphere, terrain, tubes) | Natural: the level is a sampling rate; band-limiting gives the prefiltered shape | Works, but wastes the regular structure and cannot prefilter the noise |
| Aggregates (leaves, twigs, grass) | Natural: ordered pruning with area compensation | Fails: thins out, drops disjoint parts, needed special fixes in Nanite |
| Discrete, hard-edged shapes (buildings, furniture) | No sampling rate to lower; needs semantic rules per generator or grammar rewriting | Generic, but sharp features and thin parts are where simplifiers struggle, and flat-faced models have little to remove |
| Error control | Level is defined by a parameter, error is not measured unless the generator computes it | Driven by a measured geometric error |
| Correspondence between levels | Only if the generator nests levels or emits morph targets | Collapse sequences give correspondence by construction |
| Detail above the finest level | Unlimited: evaluate the recipe again | None: needs tessellation or displacement on top |
| Storage | Recipe only; levels are derived | Levels or a collapse hierarchy must be stored or built at start-up |

- The realistic form of "one engine-wide rule" is a single screen-space target (pixels per edge or per element) plus a small contract every generator honours: accept a detail value expressed in object units (so it works per instance), produce levels at power-of-two spacings that nest, give each vertex its position in the next-coarser level, give each element an order index, and report a displacement bound. Parametric and aggregate generators can meet that contract cheaply; the owner's rocks and trees are in this class.
- Flat-faced buildings are the class where the recipe-only ideal is weakest. The evidence offers two routes: write the detail hierarchy into the recipe (mass, then storeys, then openings, then frames, each node with a projected size below which it is absorbed into its parent), which is what Besuievsky and Patow automate inside the grammar, or run a generic simplifier on the full-detail output. The first keeps the asset file a pure recipe but adds structure to it; the second keeps recipes simple but adds a tool and gives up prefiltered, semantically chosen levels.
- A hybrid mirrors the current state of the art: recipe-derived levels wherever a sampling rate or an element order exists, a generic simplifier as the fallback for anything that only emits triangles, and a far-field stand-in (cross-fade to an impostor, or a volumetric stage) below a few pixels.
- Cost of evaluating every level at start-up is modest for grids: with 2:1 nested resolutions in two dimensions the coarser levels together add one third to the work of the finest level (1/4 + 1/16 + … = 1/3), so the argument against regeneration is consistency and per-generator effort, not start-up time.

### Gaps
- No source found compares the two strategies head to head on the same assets with numbers (triangle counts, error, popping, build time). The comparison table is my synthesis.
- How well generic simplifiers cope with flat-faced, hard-edged procedural models (attribute seams, thin walls, coplanar faces) was outside what I could research before the search budget ran out; another researcher's notes on mesh simplification should be consulted for that half of the comparison.
- I did not open Besuievsky and Patow's paper; how much of a building's rule set their rewriting handles automatically, and its failure cases, are unverified.
- No source found describes a real-time engine that derives all levels of buildings from a recipe with one universal rule; the closest are grammar-level LOD attributes written by hand.
- Furniture and other non-parametric props were not covered by any source found.
